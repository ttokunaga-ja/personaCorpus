# Dues and terms of entry

LHT-20250423-PAP-001 | 23 April 2025 | Judith Morris | Comparative research seminar, 29 April 2025 | v1.0

## 1. At the door and in the room

Begin with a guest, not a price. In the April 1887 rules of the Japanese monthly reading group, a guest introduced by a member could read books on the meeting day. The rule tells us who makes the introduction and what the guest may do on that occasion. It does not, in the extract before us, prescribe a payment by the guest. The five sen stated elsewhere is a monthly subscription for members. [1]

The February 1874 rules of a York members' reading room describe an evening guest accompanied by a member, who signs for the guest. Here the surviving extract tells us about accompaniment and signature. It should not be silently expanded into a statement identical to the Japanese provision for reading. The two cases can be placed beside one another because each makes a member responsible for a relation to a guest; the place, occasion and action specified must remain visible. [2]

This is a comparison of two provisions, not of two national systems. It tells us how a guest's relation to a member was formulated in each selected rule. It does not tell us how many guests appeared, whether every prescribed signature was made, or whether the two rooms were equally accessible to people without a member to introduce them.

## 2. Taking a book away

A second scene changes the person and the act. The Japanese extract reserves borrowing outside the room to members. The York rule restricts that borrowing to members whose subscriptions have been paid. Both concern a member taking a book away; only the York clause in the material before us explicitly adds a paid-up condition. We should not supply the same condition on the Japanese side just to complete a symmetrical table. [1, 2]

The distinction between reading in a room and removing a book also matters in a French letter used here as a check on our categories, not as a third case in the comparison. In CR/14/A f. 186r the secretary permits a member in arrears to consult books on the premises in lines 3–4. Lines 5–9 deal with collecting the outstanding subscription before a book leaves and handing the money to the treasurer. The condition for removal does not turn the preceding permission into a charge for reading in the room. [3]

Keeping those acts separate makes the comparison more precise. In each rule we can ask who is addressed, what action is permitted and what qualification is stated. A rule can answer those questions without showing that a loan occurred. An account or a register may supply a different kind of evidence, but only within the limits of its own entries.

Corresponding passage in the 28-page paper: §3, p. 15.

## 3. Dues and the surviving entries

The Japanese rule prescribes five sen each month; the York rule prescribes six pence each quarter. A simple annual extension gives sixty sen and twenty-four pence. These are calculated amounts under uninterrupted payment of the stated rates, not observed annual payments. Neither currency conversion alone nor the annual figures tell us which subscription was easier to pay: payment timing, any relief, and the sums' relation to household resources are not established on matching evidence. [1, 2]

The Japanese May 1887 cash account has seven dues receipt entries. One entry explicitly covers May and June and totals ten sen. It is one entry for two months. The seven entries cannot be translated into seven May payers, and the account has no guest or visitor column in the material examined. It is not an attendance list. [4]

For York, the March 1874 attendant's sheets examined cover four evenings. Their guest column has marks made by the attendant, but no guest names and no accompanying members' signatures. This is a different record from the one in which the rule calls for signature. We cannot recognise a returning guest or connect a mark to an individual in the subscription book. Four evenings identify the span examined, not four visitors. [5]

The receipt entries and the attendant's marks do not share an observational unit. Each is evidence of a recording practice; neither is a count of distinct people attending. To investigate use person by person would require dated attendance evidence and identifiers that can be linked to the relevant financial record. The present comparison does not supply them.

Corresponding passage in the 28-page paper: §2, p. 9; see also §4 on the limits of linking records.

## 4. What evidence would an argument about influence require?

The York rule is earlier than the Japanese rule, but chronology and resemblance do not establish a route between them. A comparison of provisions can be worthwhile without a claim that one was derived from the other. To ask about direct influence, the first question is whether the relevant British wording was available to the people concerned before the Japanese rule was adopted in April 1887. A dated translation naming its recipient and source, together with a dispatch and matching receipt identifying the contents, could help establish that access. A translation alone does not prove delivery; a receipt for an unspecified parcel does not identify its text. [6]

Access and consideration are separate questions. Drafting or adoption minutes identifying the clause and the text placed before those deciding the rule could show that the provision entered their deliberations. Such minutes would still need to be read alongside any other texts considered and the wording adopted. Abe's inquiry has not found the necessary pre-adoption translation or receipt and records of the relevant consideration in the material examined. We therefore have a question for further work, not a demonstrated influence. [6]

For discussion: which surviving records could connect the introduction of a guest to an actual occasion, and what would distinguish an available text from a text considered by the drafters? These questions keep the comparison of rules separate from claims about use and transmission.

Corresponding passage in the 28-page paper: §6, pp. 25–26.
