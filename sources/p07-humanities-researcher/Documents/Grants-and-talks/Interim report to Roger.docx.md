## Evidence and outstanding checks

### Chapter 1 · Scope and sources

The opening defines the unit of comparison by asking whether the sources identify a body that had responsibility for arranging collective reading. This gives the later chapters something they can identify from different kinds of paper. A rule that specifies an officer's responsibilities and a notice that gives the hours of a room can both help establish who arranged the activity. A consignee's address documents a destination. Connecting that address with a reading body would still require evidence that the same body acted there.

The three Saint-Aubin inventories put that distinction into practice. In Box 12, CR/12/A contains rules and notices and CR/12/B contains the collection and account papers. In Box 13, material on invitations and entry falls under A, with duty and use records under B. The sections of Box 14 contain arrears and relief papers and records of arrangements and hours. The source note therefore leads from the kind of claim to the portion of the holdings that can support it. It also tells the reader where the record is interrupted.

When you checked 63 Chapter 1 references on 10 July 2025, nine led only to citations in other works. I moved those nine out of the text to the list for further investigation. A description of a rule in a later account may tell me which series to ask for. To use the rule as evidence in the opening I need a recoverable reference to the text that states the provision. The chapter's definition and the distribution of material across the boxes can be followed from the sources it now cites.

At the November reading I will check the cross-references to this definition, particularly where “reading society” is used near the discussion of ledger destinations. The opening gives the criterion for recognising a reading body. The discussion of how copies were supplied uses the unit that the ledger actually records.

### Chapter 2 · The date of LA-042

The July copy from Camille Laurent shows both faces of CR/14/A f.184. LA-042 is my reference for the passage in the chapter. The call number to use for the document is CR/14/A f.184r–v. Julie and I reopened the question of the last figure in the written year on 8 July. The old reading was 1864. On the recto, the end of the figure descends obliquely, like the 7 in the account-book reference “17” in the fourth line of the body. The date line is separate from the line count. A comparison within the same hand gave us a reading of 1867.

The writer supplied the weekday as well as the number of the day: “jeudi 16 mai”. In 1867, 16 May fell on a Thursday. In 1864 it fell on a Monday. The weekday therefore supports the reading of the figure. On the verso a different hand wrote “17 mai 1867”. This is the receipt endorsement. It places receipt of the note a day after the date on the face and provides a further check on the year. For the date of the instruction itself I cite the recto.

The instruction tells the attendant that one book may be taken out on this occasion, asks for its return the following Thursday and directs that the outstanding account stay with the treasurer. What is dated 16 May 1867 is the instruction authorising the exception. The endorsement on the verso acknowledges the note. Neither face records a completed return of the book. The revised note gives the two dates their distinct functions so that the endorsement cannot inadvertently become a date for a loan or for the return of a book.

The correction puts LA-042 after the January and March 1866 instructions used in the chapter. The spelling-out of the evidential sequence in the two revised paragraphs follows from that change.

### Chapter 6 · Limits of comparison

The April 1887 rules for the Japanese monthly group and the February 1874 rules for the York members' room give a comparison of provisions. In the former, an introduction by a member permits a guest to read on the meeting day. In York a member must sign for and accompany an evening guest. The Japanese clause reserves borrowing outside the room to members. The York clause specifies membership with dues paid. The extracts give a monthly subscription of five sen and a quarterly subscription of sixpence, respectively. The Japanese guest's payment conditions are unconfirmed.

The records of transactions and of duties qualify what can be said about the operation of those provisions. The Japanese cash account for May has seven receipt entries, one covering May and June. The York sheets cover four evenings in March. The marks in the guest column carry neither names nor accompanying members' signatures. This evidence gives two different observations: entries made for money received and the span of the attendant's surviving record examined. Neither allows a count of distinct payers or visitors. The provisions alone cannot establish a particular visit or loan.

To take a further step from comparison to influence, I would first have to identify evidence of access to the relevant British wording before adoption in April 1887. A dated translation with an identifiable source and addressee, or matching dispatch and receipt details identifying the enclosure, could establish that the text was available to those drawing up the rules. A receipt stamp with the contents unspecified leaves that part open. I would then need evidence that they took the particular clause into consideration, such as minutes naming the text and the clause discussed. The two checks answer different questions and neither is satisfied by dating the rules alone.

By 30 October I will settle the scope of the chapter in the light of the September findings. The clause comparison can stand on the extracts. The present evidence leaves direct influence open.
