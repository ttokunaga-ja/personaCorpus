# Provincial reading societies: working outline

LHT-20240425-OUT-001 | 26 April 2024 | Judith Morris  
Circulation: Roger Hewitt | Version 0.3

Roger, following our discussion in York, I have kept the six-chapter frame but moved the comparison of dues and participation to the beginning of the argument. The route of print remains important, though it can no longer stand in for the work of establishing who could use a society and who actually did. Chapter 1 defines the units and the surviving sources; chapter 2 then asks what those sources allow us to compare about the terms and practice of participation.

## 1. Scope and sources

Define the provincial reading society as the unit of comparison and explain how the surviving rules, accounts, notices and correspondence bear on that unit. Distinguish the institution responsible for a collection from the place at which people read. A shared address alone will not establish that a lending destination and a gathering are the same institution. Set out the uneven survival of records before moving from the stated terms of membership to the question of use in chapter 2.

## 2. Dues and participation

How did the terms on which dues were required and collected relate to participation, and how far can that relationship be compared between societies? Read the rules for the stated obligation separately from accounts or receipts showing collection. Look independently for attendance or use in surviving records, and for letters describing exceptions or remission. A payment is not itself a visit, just as an invitation is not proof that its recipient came. Compare amounts and practices only where the records and their units permit it; do not infer a difference in attendance from a difference in dues alone.

## 3. Rooms and opening hours

Examine the premises, opening notices and arrangements for keeping rooms open. Ask when and on what terms a room was available and who was responsible for it. Opening hours describe opportunities to attend; they do not count visits. Keep changes in access and hours distinct from the financial terms considered in chapter 2, while noting where they complicate the comparison.

## 4. Print and supply

Return to the production, acquisition and distribution of printed matter: orders, suppliers, catalogues and surviving indications of circulation. Separate evidence that a title was available or sent from evidence that a society acquired it, and distinguish an inventory entry from a trace of use. This chapter can explain routes of supply without treating them as a measure of participation.

## 5. Committee correspondence

Read committee letters for the decisions and practical arrangements behind the rules: collection, access, exceptions and the work of those responsible for the room. Ask who had authority to decide and to whom instructions were addressed. A letter may illuminate a particular decision without proving that the same practice applied everywhere or throughout the period.

## 6. Limits of comparison

Bring the cases together with their evidential differences visible. Specify which comparisons rest on rules, which on accounts and which on traces of use, and where the surviving material leaves a question open. Unequal survival or an unidentified recording unit may prevent a direct comparison; absence from a surviving entry is not automatically evidence of absence from the room.
