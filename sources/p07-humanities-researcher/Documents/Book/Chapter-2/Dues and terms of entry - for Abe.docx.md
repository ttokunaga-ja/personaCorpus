# Dues and terms of entry

LHT-20250327-CHP-001 | v0.2 | 22 April 2025 | Judith Morris | For Yukiko Abe

Comparative research seminar, 29 April 2025

## Page 1 — §1 Scope and sources

A member might owe money, bring another person into the room or ask to take away a book. Each transaction brought a different relation into view. A subscription was owed to the society. A guest came through someone already entitled to use the room. A member requesting a loan asked the society to let a book leave its custody. The terms attached to one transaction tell us only so much about the other two. This distinction is the point of setting two small bodies of material alongside one another.

I take one monthly reading group in Japan, represented by rules of April 1887 and a cash account for May, and one members’ reading room in York, represented by rules of February 1874 and attendant’s sheets from March. Abe supplied the Japanese extracts and has checked the scope of the clauses with me. I work from my own York extracts. [1–4, 7]

This is a separate paper for our discussion on 29 April. The February chapter for Ainsworth deals at length with the French correspondence. Two of those letters appear here because they help to read a condition in context. The evidence compared in this paper remains the Japanese and York material.

## Page 2 — What the April extract contains

Abe’s extract follows the order of the Japanese rules and marks the omissions. It begins with a member’s liability to pay five sen every month. Further on, it permits guests introduced by members to consult books on the day of the regular meeting. A third passage limits loans outside the room to members. The intervals between these passages matter. Reading them as one continuous instruction would make it easy to carry the subject or condition of one clause over into the next. [1]

I retain the description “monthly reading group” and take the wording of each provision from the extract. The subscription clause itself supplies the monthly interval. The guest clause supplies the occasion for reading, the meeting day. There is no reason to use the interval in the first clause to interpret how often any particular guest might be received under the second.

The May cash account is a separate source even where it uses the same word for dues. Its columns are concerned with amounts coming in and the particulars attached to an amount. It gives a view of how money was entered in the month examined. The extract of the rules gives the amount a member was meant to pay. Bringing the sources together requires keeping these two functions visible. [3]

## Page 3 — The York pair

The York rules of February 1874 set the subscription at six pence a quarter. Their evening provision requires a member to accompany a guest and sign for that guest. The provision for loans outside the room applies to members whose subscriptions have been paid. The signature belongs with the person who brings the guest. The subscription qualification belongs with the member who proposes to take a book away. [2]

The attendant’s March sheets use a guest column. The entries examined are marks put there by the attendant. The sheets lack fields for guest names and for accompanying members’ signatures. The requirement in the rules and the marks on these sheets are evidence of two different acts of writing. I can describe the prescribed signature from the rules and describe the attendant’s entries from the sheets. I cannot inspect the conduct of particular members by assigning one of those marks to a signature. [4]

The two sources are close in date. That proximity makes them worth reading together, but tells us little about what was copied from one administrative record into another. A field shared across the records would make a difference to the enquiry. The month heading alone supplies no such field.

## Page 4 — The question that travels between the cases

In each set of rules I ask who is addressed, what the person wants to do, and when the restriction takes effect. For dues the person is a member and the time is a payment interval. For a guest the setting is a particular occasion, the regular meeting in the Japanese extract and the evening admission procedure in York. For borrowing the action is the removal of a book from the room. Each comparison therefore begins with a clause and its scope. [1, 2]

The relation between the guest and the member survives this test as a subject of comparison. Both arrangements require some involvement by a member, with different procedures and different descriptions of what the guest may do. The restriction on loans also survives because both sets of rules address the same act. What falls away is a comparison of the frequency with which a condition was met. The surviving cash and duty records record different things. [3, 4]

This approach also leaves room for differences between the two institutions. A monthly group’s rule about reading at a meeting need say very little about an evening guest’s arrival at a staffed reading room. That difference is part of what the wording allows us to observe.

## Page 5 — Reading with the surviving record in view

A rule states the conduct an institution set down. The way a person met or negotiated a requirement may be visible in an entry or letter made for another purpose. To connect the two, one must be able to say what action the entry records. A receipt can show that money was taken. A direction from a secretary can show what an attendant was instructed to do in a particular case. Both can change the reading of a provision, provided the connection can be made on the page. [3, 5, 6]

The French examples help at just this point. One letter invites members to bring a companion and invites an offering towards heating. The following sentence tells us whether the offering was a term of entry. A different letter addresses an unpaid subscription and gives separate directions about reading in the room and taking a book home. There is a contextual check in each letter, and each check depends on reading more than the phrase for payment. The letters supply neither a third case in the table nor a connection between the other two. [5, 6]

I begin with the wording and account evidence on subscriptions before turning to guests and books. The distinction between the receipt and the visit will then be available when we read the duty sheets.

## Page 6 — §2 Dues and receipts

The rules give the amount and the recurring interval for the two subscriptions. In the Japanese extract the subject of the sentence is the member, with payment of five sen required every month. In York the comparable prescription is six pence per quarter. The most direct comparison is between these statements of obligation. The amounts belong with their original units and intervals, since the cycle is part of the obligation the institution described. [1, 2]

A comparison framed as “the cost of a visit” would require a different source. The two sums recur with membership. The Japanese guest provision refers to introduction and the regular meeting day. The York evening provision requires accompaniment and a signature. Neither of these particular clauses assigns the subscription amount to the arrival of a guest. It is worth keeping the figure beside the noun it modifies, even in a short account of the rules.

The Japanese evidence happens to include entries for money received shortly after the date of the rules. This gives some purchase on collection, although it is a view of one month’s entries. For York, the additional item before us is a duty record. There is no matching series of York subscription receipts in this comparison from which I could describe the collection of the six pence. [3, 4]

## Page 7 — Putting the intervals on a common footing

The arithmetic can be stated briefly. Twelve monthly liabilities of five sen make sixty sen over an uninterrupted year. Four quarterly liabilities of six pence make twenty-four pence on the same assumption. These are annual extensions of the stipulated amounts. The first says what twelve instalments at the prescribed rate would add to. The second does the same for four instalments. Neither calculation requires an account entry. [1, 2]

I retain the calculation to make the difference in intervals intelligible. A quarterly sum and a monthly sum each place a demand on a member’s resources at particular times. A household might face the two demands quite differently even if their annual equivalents could somehow be put on a common scale. For that enquiry we would need comparable payment dates, evidence of relief or delays, and some basis for locating the payments in household budgets. A conversion between currencies would still leave each of those questions open.

For discussion it is sufficient to give the ordinary amount as written, with the interval attached. I would leave the annual extension in the margin of the economic question. It describes the rule projected over a year and gives no ranking of the ease of attendance at the two rooms.

## Page 8 — The particulars of a receipt

Abe identifies the combined receipt through three elements of the account: the date field for May, the particulars specifying dues for May and June, and the amount of ten sen. The month under which the money is recorded and the months covered by it do different work. The first fixes the entry in the account as examined. The others are part of the description attached to the payment. [3]

The ten sen agrees with two monthly sums of five sen. The particulars make the two-month reading possible. An amount twice the prescription would have been weaker evidence in isolation because a bare figure leaves the periods covered unclear. The value of this line is that the account itself attaches the months to the money. That is also the reason to keep it intact when counting receipt entries.

Abe has supplied that row as an extract. I use her count of the other six entries and leave their detailed allocation unstated here. To recover the pattern of payment over successive months I would need to read the particulars of each receipt with the rest of the account. This is a closer question than the broader one of whether the group met or a given person came to read.

## Page 9 — The rate and the seven entries

The Japanese rules prescribe **five sen per member per month**. The May 1887 cash account contains **seven entries recording receipt of dues**. One is a single receipt of ten sen explicitly covering May and June. The other six bring the account count to seven. York’s prescribed sum is six pence per quarter and comes from the February 1874 rule. The March York sheets are a record of duty and add no receipt total to this passage. [1–4]

| Evidence | Amount or count | Unit and coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese rules, April 1887 | Five sen | Prescribed monthly subscription for a member |
| Japanese cash account, May 1887 | Seven | Dues-receipt entries entered in May |
| Combined receipt within the seven | Ten sen | One entry covering May and June |
| York rules, February 1874 | Six pence | Prescribed quarterly subscription for a member |

“Entered in May” describes the place of the seven receipts in the account. It leaves open how many distinct members paid for May, which entries referred to that month’s liability and whether someone who paid later came to the room. The account gives no guest or visitor field and we have no attendance register that can be matched to the receipts. A visit total would require evidence made to record visits. [3, 7]

## Page 10 — From dues owed to dues taken

A rule, a demand and a receipt may all refer to the same obligation at different stages. The document establishing the recurring sum says what the member should pay. A demand could show that someone was asked to pay it by a certain time. A receipt is evidence of money taken. It matters whether the question concerns entitlement on the day, the member’s outstanding account or the treasurer’s record of money actually received.

The March comparison section distinguished the entries for money received from the amounts prescribed by the two rules. Here the combined receipt also shows how the period of account differs from the period of liability. Once the two periods are distinguished, it becomes possible to ask which of them a later count is supposed to represent. Counting the ten sen as two receipts would change the unit halfway through the account. Treating the seven entries as seven members would require the persons to be identified. [3, 8]

With an account spanning further months and supplying the necessary particulars, one could try to assign a receipt to the relevant liability. That reconstruction would still leave attendance to be sought elsewhere. The May extract establishes that the account can group two dues periods under one receipt, which is already enough to qualify a count based on the month heading.

## Page 11 — What the subscription clause can explain

The prescription establishes membership as a recurring financial relation in each case. It provides a way into the institutional question of how a room was maintained and who was expected to contribute. It has a second, more precise use in York, where payment status is expressly made a condition of borrowing outside. To use it there we must have the two provisions before us, the one stating the rate and the other stating the condition. [2]

There is no corresponding paid-up wording in the Japanese loan clause as excerpted; the named qualification there is membership. Even someone who has paid the ordinary rate may have acted as member, introducer or borrower on different occasions. The subscription is therefore relevant to the loan comparison where the clause says so, while a guest’s reception must be followed through the guest provision. [1, 2]

The cash account does show that dues were being received in May. Its silence about who entered the room is readily explained by what its columns were made to record. That is a better starting point for considering the gap than assuming that all the obligations or privileges in the rules will leave equivalent entries in a ledger. I turn next to the provisions whose subjects and occasions can be compared directly.

## Page 12 — §3 Guests and borrowing

The guest provisions both bring a member into the encounter between the institution and someone outside its membership. In the Japanese case the rule grants reading on the regular meeting day to a guest introduced by a member. In York the member is to sign for and accompany an evening guest. The Japanese passage names the reading for which the guest is admitted. The York wording before us describes a procedure for the guest’s arrival. [1, 2]

My March draft spoke too broadly of “participation” in bringing these arrangements together. In our discussion of 2 April Abe and I put the guest in the room first, then the member wishing to take a book away, and placed actual use in a separate category. The order makes one ask about the book and the person in each clause before making a general statement about access. Roger’s remarks also drew attention to what was being counted in the records of use. [7, 8]

I would still use “terms of entry” for the question as a whole. Within it the member’s introduction and the York member’s signature can be compared as specified procedures, and each procedure can be described in the setting the source gives it.

## Page 13 — Introduction on the regular meeting day

The words Abe has excerpted are 「会員ノ紹介アル客」, a guest having a member’s introduction. The rest of that passage permits consultation of books on the day of the regular meeting. I keep the expression “introduced by a member” in the translation. It leaves open the means of introduction and avoids carrying the written signature or personal accompaniment from the York rule into the Japanese passage. [1]

The operation of the clause can be stated with some precision even where the procedure is underspecified. It identifies the guest through the member and identifies the occasion by the meeting day. The provision for members’ ordinary dues occurs elsewhere in the extract. Abe found no sum assigned to the guest in the passages examined. There is consequently no basis here for adding five sen or for claiming that all guests entered free under any circumstances. [1, 7]

The scope of 閲覧 is equally important. Here it concerns consultation of books in the setting of the meeting. “Borrow” would suggest a power over the book beyond what the passage gives, especially when the separate loan provision addresses removal. I retain “read in the room” for the act under discussion and leave the loan clause to its own subject.

## Page 14 — Signature and accompaniment in York

The February York rule makes the member responsible for two actions at the evening arrival. The member accompanies the guest and signs for the guest. In the form in which we have it, the obligation to sign belongs to the member. The guest’s own name and what the guest did after entry remain separate matters to be sought in records capable of telling us those things. [2]

Signature supplies a point at which an attendant might recognise the member’s responsibility. The rule is good evidence for the procedure contemplated. To ask whether a specific member followed it on a particular evening would require a record in which the signature survived. In the March duty sheets that I have examined, the guest column is filled with attendant’s marks and lacks a field for the member’s signature. That difference should be kept visible when the two York documents are cited together. [4]

The shared feature with the monthly group is that a member mediates the guest’s reception. The York rule gives an evening procedure in which the member’s presence and signature are specified. The Japanese rule gives an introduction for reading on a meeting day. A short gloss such as “member sponsorship” can introduce the parallel, but it has to be followed by both descriptions.

## Page 15 — The guest in the room and the member with a loan

In the April 1887 Japanese rules, a guest introduced by a member may read on the regular meeting day. A different clause limits loans outside the room to members. The **guest’s reading in the room** and the **member’s taking a book away** are separate acts, with separate qualifications. The monthly five sen is the member’s subscription. The extract leaves any guest payment unstated. [1]

In York an evening guest is to come with a member who signs for the guest. The rule on taking a book away applies to paid-up members. Payment status thus qualifies the loan in the York text. The guest clause describes accompaniment and signature. It gives us no warrant to shift the member’s borrowing condition to the guest at the door. [2]

| Act | Japanese monthly reading group | York members’ reading room |
|---|---|---|
| Guest use under the provision | Member’s introduction for reading on the regular meeting day | Member signs and accompanies the evening guest |
| Loan outside the room | Members only | Members whose subscriptions are paid |

The two loan clauses are comparable as restrictions on removal. The Japanese extract specifies membership. The York version adds paid-up status. The surviving Japanese wording leaves the treatment of arrears in practice open. I would keep this page beside the receipts on p. 9 when discussing whether introduction and payment are the same sort of condition.

## Page 16 — A Saturday invitation and its following sentence

The secretary’s letter of 28 October 1862 invites members to a Saturday reading. Each member may bring one other person. Members and guests may make an *offrande libre* towards heating, and the next sentence expressly welcomes those who give nothing. The three statements appear together in **CR/13/A f. 085r**, lines 3–5. [5]

The assurance about those who give nothing settles how I read the offering in this invitation. Its place beside the clause about the companion can give the impression of a price for the occasion if the next sentence is omitted. The letter itself retains the arrangement for bringing a companion while giving members and guests discretion over the gift. The warming of the room was a purpose for which money was invited. Entry under this invitation was available without that gift.

This is the comparison I want to bring back from the French reading. The following sentence can tell us which condition actually attaches to an act. Here we have an explicit welcome without payment. The Japanese guest clause is silent about an amount. The two evidential situations are different, and the confidence permitted by the French letter about this particular gift cannot be transferred to the Japanese case.

## Page 17 — When the book is about to leave

The French secretary’s letter of 19 March 1866 concerns a member in arrears. The instruction to allow him to continue consulting books in the room appears in **CR/14/A f. 186r**, lines 3–4. Lines 5–9 describe what to do if he asks to take out a book. The attendant is to receive the outstanding subscription before the book leaves and then hand over the money to the treasurer with the member’s name. [6]

The specified point of collection matters. It is reached when a member who is allowed to consult the books asks to remove one. The secretary’s direction would be distorted by describing the payment as a charge at the entrance, since the preceding permission to read in the room would then disappear. The handover instruction helps identify the payment as dues collected on behalf of the society.

York’s paid-up qualification also belongs to the clause on loans outside. I cite the French letter to make the difference between consultation and removal easy to see, while keeping its treatment of this particular member within the letter. The York rule gives a general qualification for the borrower. Neither document, by itself, describes how often the qualification arose in practice. [2, 6]

## Page 18 — §4 Traces of use

The duty sheets that I examined cover four evenings in March 1874. The guest column gives marks made by the attendant. Because there is no name attached to a mark, a later evening offers no way of recognising a returning guest. The information preserved by the sheets is the attendant’s recording of occasions within the four evenings examined. “Four” refers to the evenings represented by the sheets. A count of distinct guests would require information of a different order. [4]

The February rule says that members sign for the guests they accompany. There is no place to read the signatures in this guest column. The duty sheets can tell us about the attendant’s record, but they leave the evidence for the prescribed signature to another record. It would be just as unsafe to infer from the blank in our evidence that the practice of signing was abandoned as to put a particular member’s signature behind each mark. [2, 4]

This is where the point about what the institution intended to record becomes practical. A mark allowed the attendant to put something down under “guest” during a shift. A historian asking how often a particular person came needs an identifier that persists across shifts. The column examined supplies none.

## Page 19 — Why the records do not join by person

Abe’s May cash extract has a date, particulars and amount. Those are good fields for describing when money was taken and which liability a receipt covered when the particulars specify it. The fields examined contain no record of a person’s attendance. An attendance register for the corresponding meetings would be necessary even to begin matching the money to visits. We would then have to establish who each entry referred to, with enough information to tell a repeat visit from the presence of a second person. [3, 7]

In York the attempted link would run between a guest mark on the duty sheets and the member who brought the guest, then between that member and the subscription book. The sheets give neither a guest name nor an accompanying member’s signature. Even if the subscription book distinguished every member cleanly, the guest column provides no shared identifier to start the match. The evening date cannot do the work of a name. [4]

The Japanese and York figures therefore present separate problems of linkage within each institution. They also concern different years, different populations and different kinds of event. Setting them next to one another cannot supply the missing links within either case.

## Page 20 — What a frequency would require

A rate of visiting among members who had paid would require membership and payment status at a known date, visits that can be attributed to the same people, and a period of observation common to both kinds of evidence. The denominator would consist of people eligible to be observed under the stated definition. It would also be necessary to know what an absent visit record meant for that period. A similar denominator and observation window would be needed for members in arrears.

The May account supplies neither the visit entries nor the complete membership and status information needed for those denominators. The York guest marks do not identify attending members or link an accompanying member to an account. Counting the number of evenings in York cannot supply the number of people eligible to attend. Counting the Japanese entries cannot supply the number who paid in respect of any one month. [3, 4]

Were the requisite records found, a comparison between the institutions would also need a decision about which event to call a visit. Reading at a monthly meeting and arriving as an evening guest have different occasions and populations. Establishing rates within each institution would be a first step. Any comparison of those rates would be a further enquiry.

## Page 21 — What can still be said about practice

The May entry for ten sen gives a small but definite piece of administrative practice. A single receipt covered two months of dues. This tells us how a payment could be recorded under the monthly system. It does not tell us why those months were paid together or when the relevant member chose to come to read. Any of those questions would need evidence about the person or the meetings beyond the particulars of the entry. [3]

The York marks give a second kind of evidence. They show that the attendant put marks in a guest column on the surviving sheets examined. This is consistent with guests being a category the room’s keepers had occasion to record. The column leaves open the number of distinct visitors, how often they returned and whether the signatures required by the rule can be found elsewhere. [4]

I would report both observations in the terms of the records that support them. By doing so I can retain something about the working room alongside its written rules. I can also explain why combining a number of receipts with a number of evenings would be of no help. The next table places each observation beside its documentary footing so that the two can be discussed together on those terms.

## Page 23 — Where the parallel holds

I would retain three comparisons for the seminar. First, both sets of rules lay down a regular contribution from members. The practice of prescribing a subscription is common, and the time over which each payment is stipulated differs. The original amounts and intervals are enough to state that finding. An economic comparison of the burdens would call for corresponding evidence about the households concerned. [1, 2]

Secondly, both sets of rules place a member between the society and an outside reader. The Japanese words give the guest a particular activity on the regular meeting day. The York words describe accompaniment and a member’s signature for an evening guest. The member’s role is the shared element, while the procedure and its immediate purpose should remain in the description.

Thirdly, the loan clauses reserve removal of books for people with a defined relationship to the institution. Both say that the borrower is a member, and York expressly requires paid-up status. This is the closest comparison of the three because the act in question is the same. The conclusion is about the written qualification to take a book away. Extending it to payment at the door or to the borrowing history of individuals would require records of those events. [1, 2]

## Page 24 — Where the terms of comparison change

When the question shifts from entitlement to use, the rules serve as a guide to the records one might look for. A prescribed signature points towards a record capable of preserving the member’s signature. A borrowing qualification points towards a loan record and a subscription account that could be read together. Those are leads for enquiry. The rules themselves do not give a set of names with dates and payment status. [1, 2]

The administrative sources on p. 22 illustrate the gap. The May account can answer a question about the way a receipt was entered. The York sheets can answer a question about marks put in a guest column during four evenings. The account and the sheets have a place in the same argument because they show what survives of two different kinds of record. This is also why the table gives the coverage of each in its own terms. [3, 4]

A further change of question comes when a similarity is offered as evidence of historical contact. The borrowing clauses, for example, can be compared even if no text ever passed between the two institutions. To examine contact one must follow a copy, a reader and a decision. The first question is whether the relevant words could have been read in time to enter the Japanese group’s deliberations.

## Page 25 — §6 What direct influence would require

### A text available to read before adoption

The February 1874 York rules precede the April 1887 Japanese rules. For a claim about direct influence I would require evidence that someone concerned in drawing up the Japanese rules received the relevant wording and had it available to read before adoption in April 1887. The two dates give the outside chronology. They leave the intervening act of transmission unestablished. [1, 2, 7, 9]

A dated translation could supply this link if it identified the text translated and the intended recipient, and there were evidence that it reached a relevant reader in time. A dispatch identifying the enclosure and addressee, matched by a dated receipt for the same text, could establish when the words were available at the receiving end. A copy prepared before adoption could still have remained with its translator. Likewise a receipt for a parcel would need some indication that it contained these rules. The reader’s connection to the drafting would have to be shown. [9]

I have dropped the March suggestion that the likeness itself offers a clue to a route. The materials Abe and I have examined supply no dated evidence of a relevant recipient’s access to the York wording before adoption. That is a specific gap about who could read what and when. A second gap concerns what the drafters considered. [7–9]

## Page 26 — Whether the clauses came before the drafters

Even if the receipt of a copy were established, there would still be a question about how it was used. For this we would need drafting or adoption minutes that identify the text before the meeting and show which provisions were discussed. For example, a record that a particular loan clause was read and weighed as a condition on proposed lending would give a reason to set it alongside the corresponding clause in the adopted rules. Minutes recording only that rules were adopted would give the date of the decision and leave the discussion unknown. [9]

The useful sequence would run from the identified text before the drafters, through their treatment of a clause, to the terms they adopted. The minutes might show that the clause was accepted, altered or set aside. Other proposals mentioned in the discussion would have to be read in their own terms. A record that the text was circulated shows the opportunity to consider it and leaves open whether the drafters took up the clause.

The Japanese extracts before us establish the adopted conditions on dues, guest reading and external loans. They are unaccompanied here by drafting or adoption minutes recording consideration of the York wording. Both this evidence and the pre-adoption evidence on p. 25 are missing from the material examined. [1, 7, 9]

## Page 27 — The claim to retain

We can compare what the clauses make a person do before reading as a guest or borrowing as a member. We can describe the differences in the two subscription cycles and observe one receipt that covers two months. These modest claims have the advantage of leaving the documents visible in the argument. They also give us fairly exact questions to bring back to the records if the evidence broadens. [1–4]

For the question of influence I would make two separate enquiries. One would follow any dated translation, dispatch and receipt for the relevant wording before adoption. The other would look for records of drafting and adoption that could connect discussion of a provision to the words eventually used. A useful find in either line of enquiry would determine what could be asked of the other. For now the likeness between some of the clauses is a comparison of stated conditions. The route by which a text might have come into a drafter’s hands remains to be established. [7, 9]

For our discussion I suggest opening p. 15 beside p. 9. The first fixes the difference between a guest reading on the premises and a member taking a book away. The second fixes the unit of the seven Japanese entries. The distinction between the two unmet requirements on pp. 25–26 is the one I would keep in reserve for the question of direct influence.
