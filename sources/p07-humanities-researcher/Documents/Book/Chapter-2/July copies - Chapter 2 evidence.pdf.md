# July: what the copies can support

LHT-20240730-PAP-001 | 30 July 2024 | Judith Morris | For Roger Hewitt

## The chapter question and the selection

In April we moved dues and participation to the second chapter. The question is whether differences in dues can help explain differences in participation, but the terms must first be made comparable: a stated obligation, a payment and a visit are distinct observations. The request to Camille Laurent on 9 July therefore asked for rules and collection in box 12, attendance or use in box 13, and exceptions and operating correspondence in box 14. The six items selected during the visit give us a way to test that separation; they do not yet supply a comparison between societies.

The paper copies received on 24 July comprise ten leaves: three from box 12, three from box 13 and four from box 14. They are a selection from the 216 leaves the inventories identify as available to request for copying, not copies of all 216. The inventories also locate 19 missing leaves. Their contents are unknown and those intervals are excluded from the comparison below.

## Box 12: obligation and collection

The secretary's notice of 4 February 1861 sets the quarterly subscription at one franc fifty centimes, with a first deadline of 15 February. It directs payment to the treasurer and describes a receipt stub retained with the cash papers and an account sent to the secretary. It asks that people be identified when a remittance covers more than one person. This is evidence of a stated rate and procedure, not of each member's payment or attendance.¹

The treasurer's letter of 2 March 1863 reports receiving one remittance for three members. He explains that, hitherto, when money came through the duty attendant, he entered the total under the attendant's name without individual shares on the stub. He asks where to retain the name of the person bringing the money and each person's share, and will not alter the old entry before the secretary replies. The letter does not specify the name under which this particular remittance was entered, nor record an answer. It shows why a named total cannot automatically be counted as one person's payment, much less a visit.²

## Box 13: invitation, request and visit

The invitation of 5 November 1862 permits each member to bring one guest to the reading on 8 November. The postscript instructs the attendant to list companions separately from members, with the presenting member identified. The invitation and instruction show a proposed means of entry and recording; they do not establish who came.³

In the evening attendant's letter of 5 December 1865, one person requested a newspaper and then a book without leaving the room. The attendant entered two requests but explicitly counted one visit. A different line has a difficult name that he intends to check separately. Request lines, visits and identified people are therefore different units; the uncertain name cannot be matched on supposition.⁴

## Box 14: exceptions and opportunity

On 9 February 1866 the secretary told the attendant that the committee had deferred collection in one case until the end of February. Reading in the room was to continue during that delay, while requests to take books away were to be referred to the secretary. The authority, period and distinction between uses are explicit. This one decision does not establish a general remission rule or prove that the person visited.⁵

The committee's notice of 6 October 1868 orders an October trial extending Thursday opening from seven to eight in the evening while subscription conditions remain unchanged. It assigns the handover and lighting and asks for visits during the extra hour to be distinguished in the journal, without recounting anyone already present. The notice gives a possible change in opportunity at an unchanged stated dues condition; it does not give the journal's results or demonstrate increased attendance.⁶

## What can be carried into chapter 2

These copies support a chapter organised around four checks: prescribed terms, collection, traces of use and treatment of exceptions. Dates, names and place may guide a search for connections, but the items sampled here concern different dates and do not themselves link a payment to a named visit. The invitation cannot stand in for attendance, and a two-line request cannot be counted as two visits. Nor can a single deferred payment or an announced extension be treated as an outcome for the whole society.

The missing intervals are CR/12/A 018–020 and CR/12/B 061–063; CR/13/A 094–098; CR/14/A 165–168 and CR/14/B 212–215. The leaf index records each position and its source inventory. These gaps are not empty entries, and nothing about their contents can be inferred from their location. Before making a numerical comparison, we need extant records with identifiable units and coverage, and, where possible, a dated link between collection and use. Until then I would leave open whether differences in dues explain differences in participation.
