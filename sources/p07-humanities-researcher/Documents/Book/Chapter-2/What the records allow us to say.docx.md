# What the records allow us to say

LHT-20260904-CHP-001 | 4 September 2026 | Judith Morris | For discussion with Yukiko Abe | v1.0

## §1 Receipts and attendance

The May 1887 cash account of the Japanese monthly reading group has seven dues receipt entries. One entry covers both May and June. The heading in the Japanese text should say what the account records: a receipt of money. Even where the entry specifies the period paid for, it cannot be turned into a monthly headcount. The extract neither records who came into the room nor allows the receipts to be checked against an attendance list. [1]

In York the March 1874 attendant's sheets examined cover four evenings. The guest column has marks made by the attendant. There are no guest names or signatures of the accompanying members in those sheets. We cannot recognise a guest across evenings or link the marks person by person to the subscription book. The span covered by the sheets tells us how many evenings we have examined. [2]

The seven receipts and the four evenings are different units. Neither supplies a count of distinct payers or visitors. Retain the nouns after the numbers in the summary, particularly where the two examples sit in the same sentence.

## §2 Reading and borrowing

The Japanese April 1887 rule provides for a guest introduced by a member to read on the meeting day. The extract gives a monthly subscription of five sen for members and leaves the payment conditions for guests unconfirmed. The York February 1874 rule requires a member to sign for and accompany an evening guest. The subscription is sixpence a quarter. It is worth comparing the part assigned to a member in each provision while keeping the different occasions and actions visible. [1, 2]

The clauses for taking a book outside the room address members. The Japanese clause reserves borrowing to members, while the York clause specifies paid-up members. We have no basis in the Japanese extract for adding the York condition. Neither a provision for reading nor one for borrowing proves that a particular visit or loan occurred. In the Japanese summary the verbs need to stay attached to what the rules prescribe, especially where the account and the attendant's sheets are discussed next.

## §3 Resemblance and influence

Neither similarity nor chronological order establishes direct influence. The February 1874 text is earlier, but the comparison of clauses by itself tells us nothing about the route by which its wording might have reached the Japanese group. A claim of direct influence needs evidence at two points. [3]

First, establish that the relevant British wording was available to the people drawing up the Japanese rules before adoption in April 1887. Look for a dated translation identifying its source and intended recipient, or matched dispatch and receipt records that identify the enclosure and addressee. A stamp on a receipt for unspecified contents cannot identify the wording. Second, establish that the recipients considered the wording in drafting or adopting the rule. Minutes identifying the material placed before them and the clause discussed could support that step. The sources examined do not establish the two stages. End the summary with the comparison the records support and leave the question of transmission open.
