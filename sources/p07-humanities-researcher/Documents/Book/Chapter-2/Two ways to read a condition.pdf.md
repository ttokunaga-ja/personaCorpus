# Two ways to read a condition

LHT-20250410-PAP-001 | 10 April 2025 | Judith Morris | Discussion with Roger Hewitt | v1.0

## A common subject: the member borrowing

The rules permit a comparison when the subject and the act are kept in view. The April 1887 rules of the Japanese monthly reading group reserve taking a book outside to members. The February 1874 York rules reserve that act to members whose dues are paid. Both clauses concern a member taking a book away; the paid-up qualification belongs explicitly to the York clause. The difference should not be supplied on the Japanese side merely to make the entries symmetrical.

## A scene before a comparison: the guest

A Japanese member may introduce a guest to read on a meeting day. A York member signs and accompanies an evening guest. The former extract identifies reading; the latter describes the accompanying member's action. Comparing “guest participation” without this setting puts different propositions into the same column. We can compare the relation to a member and then say what each source actually specifies. The Japanese extract gives no guest payment. It does not follow that the guest paid five sen, the amount prescribed for members.

## Which example should open the talk?

The borrowing clauses give the audience an apparently stable shared subject and a clear difference in qualification. They are useful for a compact table, but they make the distinction between a person inside the room and a member taking a volume away less immediate. The guest example requires more explanation yet exposes the problem of choosing a scene: introduction, signature, reading and accompaniment are not interchangeable words. I propose to try it first with Roger, before bringing in the borrowing clauses as the second step.

## What the records add

The Japanese May cash account contains seven dues receipts, one covering May and June; the York March attendant's sheets contain guest marks for four evenings. The first records money received, the second marks made by an attendant without guest names or accompanying signatures. Neither is a count of individual visitors, and the two cannot be treated as a common measure of use. A rule is evidence of an expressed condition; a record made for a different purpose needs its own unit of observation.

Source note: Abe's working extracts of the April 1887 rules and May cash account; York reading-room rules, February 1874, and attendant's sheets, March 1874; discussion with Abe, 2 April 2025.
