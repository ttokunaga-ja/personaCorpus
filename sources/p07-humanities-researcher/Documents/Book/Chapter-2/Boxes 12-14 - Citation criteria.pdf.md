# Which evidence can travel across boxes

LHT-20250203-PAP-001 | 3 February 2025 | Judith Morris | For Roger Hewitt | v1.0

## What the citation is doing

I propose to cite each passage for what the writer had occasion to put on record. A notice gives the terms on which money was due; a letter between the attendant and the treasurer can show money, an envelope or an account passing between them. An invitation gives the proposed terms of reception, and an instruction to an attendant can distinguish the uses he is authorised to permit. These statements can be compared across boxes if the part played by each survives the comparison. Here is what would disappear if we entered each as one record of participation.

## Box 12 — the quarter's demand

The reminder on CR/12/A f. 023r, ll. 1–7, tells a member that the term has passed and that the secretary has received no notice of payment. It asks for one franc fifty to be paid to the treasurer as the quarterly subscription, keeps the lamp gift separately accounted for and promises a receipt. Cite it for the demand, its amount and the destination of payment. For how money was handled, use the correspondence in CR/12/B. At f. 037r, ll. 1–9, the treasurer asks the attendant to pass him a labelled envelope holding two francs for coal and to enter subscriptions separately. The envelope has been reported; the letter directs the next handover. A participation tally would efface both the difference between demand and handling and the difference between dues and an earmarked gift.

## Box 13 — the terms of an invitation

CR/13/A f. 085r, ll. 3–6, allows each member one companion and asks that the companion be introduced to the attendant. The heating gift is voluntary, and the welcome extends to those who give nothing. Cite the line about the companion for the scope of the invitation and the lines about the gift for how the secretary solicited help with heating. The writer is arranging a reading in advance. An entry counted as participation would take the place of a record of arrivals which the invitation never undertook to give.

## Box 14 — the point at which the account matters

The secretary's instruction in CR/14/A f. 186r, ll. 3–9, leaves consultation in the room open to a member in arrears. The attendant is to collect what is owed before letting a book out and pass that sum and the member's name to the treasurer. Cite the earlier lines for reading on the premises and the later lines for the condition on removal. The instruction explains what to do if the member asks to borrow. In a single column for participation it would be impossible to see whether our entry meant that someone read, asked for a book, paid, or took the book away.

## For the chapter

I'll keep the invitation and the borrowing condition in their own sections. Wherever I move from a direction to an account of something done, the next citation should identify the record of that act. An attendant's report can show a particular occasion of service without supplying a series of visits. A receipt can establish a payment while leaving the question of reading open. The point of putting the boxes alongside one another is to recover these different conditions and acts, with their evidence still attached.
