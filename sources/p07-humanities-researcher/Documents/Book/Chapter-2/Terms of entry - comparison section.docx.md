# Dues and terms of entry: comparison section

LHT-20250327-CHP-001 | 27 March 2025 | Judith Morris | For Yukiko Abe | v0.1

## §2 Dues and receipts

The two sets of rules give us amounts on different cycles. The Japanese monthly reading group prescribed five sen a month in April 1887; the York members' reading room prescribed six pence a quarter in February 1874. Extended over a year without interruption these would be sixty sen and twenty-four pence respectively. The resulting figures describe the rules, not the sums actually paid by particular members. They are not a scale of relative burden: payment dates, relief from payment and the place of each sum in a household budget have not been established on a comparable basis. [1, 2]

Abe's extract from the Japanese cash account gives seven entries for dues received in May 1887. One entry explicitly combines May and June and records ten sen. It is one receipt entry for two months, not two separate entries. The remaining six entries bring the total to seven; the extract does not establish that seven people paid for May. The account records money, and the columns examined do not identify guests or visitors. We cannot infer attendance at the meeting from it. [3]

The distinction matters even where the prescribed monthly sum seems to make counting straightforward. An account made to register receipts can group periods in one line. To turn those lines into monthly payers we would need the individual entries and a way to determine the period and person for each payment. To turn them into attendance we would need a further record of visits, linked by person and date. Neither operation follows from the rule itself.

## §3 Guests and borrowing

In the Japanese rule a guest introduced by a member may read books on the meeting day. The extract places this provision apart from the clause reserving loans outside the room to members. It gives no amount payable by the guest. The five sen is stated as a member's monthly subscription; it should not be attached to the guest by analogy. In York an evening guest was to be accompanied by a member who signed for the guest. The rule for taking books away, by contrast, restricted borrowing to members whose subscriptions were paid. [1, 2]

Both arrangements make a member the intermediary for a guest's participation. The Japanese extract identifies reading at a meeting; the York provision describes signature and accompaniment in the evening. These are related conditions, though the terms do not specify precisely the same action. A comparison of participation must therefore keep the setting alongside the label. In particular, a condition on a member's borrowing cannot automatically be made into a condition on a guest's entry.

The borrowing provisions have a more closely shared subject: a member taking a book outside. The Japanese extract confines that act to members; the York rule adds the condition that the member be paid up. Absence of that qualification from the Japanese extract is not proof that arrears never affected a loan. It is the limit of the provision now before us.

## §4 Traces of use

The York attendant's sheets cover four evenings in March 1874. Their guest column contains marks entered by the attendant, but has neither names of guests nor signatures of accompanying members. The signature demanded by the rule belongs to a different record. The marks are traces of occasions on which the attendant made entries; the four evenings are the span examined, not a total of distinct visitors. A returning guest cannot be recognised from these marks. [4]

Nor can the marks be linked person by person to the subscription book without a shared identifier. The Japanese cash account and the York sheets answer different administrative questions: one records receipts, the other marks in a guest column. Seven entries and four evenings are not equivalent measures. The comparison is strongest at the level of the expressed conditions and much less secure when it asks how often those conditions were exercised.

A French letter helps to keep two acts apart. On 19 March 1866 the secretary allowed a member in arrears to continue consulting books in the room; he instructed the attendant to receive the outstanding subscription before allowing a book to be taken away and to pass the money to the treasurer. The permission for consultation is in CR/14/A f. 186r, lines 3–4; the instruction concerning arrears and handover is in lines 5–9. The letter distinguishes the place and act, not a general tariff for access. [5]

## §6 Similarity and a possible route

The York rule predates the Japanese rule by thirteen years. Their shared attention to a member's relation to a guest and to loans outside the room offers a possible clue to a route by which rules travelled. That resemblance calls for closer inquiry rather than an assertion that the Japanese group copied York. We do not yet have evidence that its drafters received or read the York wording before adoption, nor a record of their discussion of it. The comparison can describe the provisions without assuming identical practice.
