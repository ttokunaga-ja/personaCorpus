# Dues and participation

Chapter 2

LHT-20250212-CHP-001 | 26 February 2025 | Judith Morris | For Edward Fairclough, Ainsworth Scholarly Press | v1.1

## §1 Scope of the comparison

The unit of comparison in this chapter is the condition recorded in a document of the Société de lecture de Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive. I compare the terms on which dues were to be paid, a guest could accompany a member, a book could be consulted in the room or a volume could be taken away. A secretary writing about money overdue specifies what the society expects of the person addressed. A treasurer directing the transfer of an envelope wants it to arrive with enough information to make an entry. An attendant reporting a reader's requests supplies evidence of work done in the room, against which the arrangements for use can be read. Each paper is cited for the condition or act its writer had occasion to record. A member's appearance in one of these papers gives no general account of that person's use of the society.

Boxes 12 to 14 in the Archives de Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive furnish the principal examples. I began with three mornings of reading in July 2024. The arrangement of the boxes suggested a sequence from rules and finance through invitations and use to exceptional decisions and daily arrangements. The catalogue divides each box into two groups. In box 12 the first comprises rules and notices, the second collection and settlement. In box 13 invitation and entrance precede the papers of attendance on duty and use. Box 14 brings decisions on delays and arrears into proximity with papers on operating the room. The classification is a guide to finding material, and individual letters readily cross its boundaries. The shelfmarks and sequence of numbered leaves are more useful for citation than the broad catalogue descriptions. [1]

A letter about a lamp can make the distinction between a gift and a subscription clearer than a rule that deals with subscriptions alone. An instruction filed with arrears can tell us which kind of reading the society was prepared to allow before the account was cleared. Conversely, a financial entry may say less about a member's opportunities in the room than a notice addressed to people who might never have come. I have kept the recipient and immediate purpose of each paper in view wherever that recipient can be ascertained. When the writer communicates a committee decision, the instruction has a different source of authority from the writer's report that an envelope has arrived.

The material within reach changed during the writing. In July I took ten leaves in six sets of copies, including a collection paper in box 12, two invitation leaves and a report returned with an evening register in box 13. In September the archive supplied thirty-two letters, each on one leaf. Julie Perrin and I divided the first reading and then checked the transcriptions together in October. That was the stage at which the terminology of the payments became least safe to gloss by a single English word. The January copies, twelve further leaves in pairs, called for a different operation, the identification of passages with variant names used in arrangements for meetings and deliveries. [2]

The choice of these sources was governed by questions about what the papers would let us compare. The February 1863 envelopes form an especially useful sequence because one can follow a reported deposit into instructions about the label and onwards to a statement that envelopes were received. The October 1862 invitation belongs beside instructions for arranging seats and receiving companions, which supply some material setting for a short notice. Neither sequence provides a continuous history of the society between those dates. Juxtaposition is most revealing when intervals and changes of writer remain visible. It allows an expectation phrased in one letter to be tested against what another writer had occasion to report.

The figures used below serve different ends. Sums in francs and centimes identify particular demands, gifts and balances. Leaf numbers enable the reader to find the words and see where a quotation falls within a document. Counts from the January comparison describe the number of occurrences examined during a particular reading. The account of participation has no equivalent series of figures. Even within a single evening, dividing an attendant's entries by the number of names one can read can misstate how many people came. A remark explaining the entries may give better evidence for a single visit than the aggregate of the marks in the register. [3]

I use the French terms where the argument turns on the distinction that the writer makes. A prescription followed by a reason, or an amount followed by the account to which it belongs, is generally more revealing than an isolated noun. In the notes each line number begins with the first line of the body of the particular letter. Dates and salutations are outside that count. I refer to copies by shelfmark and face. The transcriptions supply the context in which the translations were settled, including a proposed reading where the copy leaves a word in doubt. Images of the reading copies are omitted.

The chapter follows the movement from assessments to actual handling, then turns to the terms of invitations and the use of books. Money directed to the treasurer and money offered to warm the room enter the account for different reasons. The chapters on rooms and book supply can subsequently take up the material setting that is only visible here where it crosses one of those transactions. The final sections consider what can travel from one set of papers to another when a name or an apparent counterpart invites a connection. The prospect of comparison depends on keeping the grounds for each connection in sight.

## §2 Assessing and collecting dues

An assessment sets out what is due. A reminder calls on a member to discharge that obligation, and a receipt or a completed account entry can establish that money was received. On 4 March 1861 the secretary asked a member to put his account in order. The term fixed for payment had passed. The secretary had yet to receive word of payment and directed the member to give the treasurer the quarterly subscription of one franc fifty centimes as soon as possible. He explained that the offering for the lamp was accounted for separately and that the treasurer would issue the receipt for the subscription. I count the letter at f. 023r as a reminder. The promise shows how payment was to be acknowledged. Establishing that this member actually paid would require the corresponding receipt or an account entry recording settlement. [4]

The amount in the letter is intelligible through its date and the expression *cotisation du trimestre*. To call it a donation would give the member discretion that the request to regularise his account plainly does not contemplate. The sum is stated in full, without a calculation of a share of expenditure or a question about what the member can spare. The secretary's reference to the lamp provides a local check on the terminology. He has an offering to account for and does not use that word for the quarterly sum. The passage is useful because the partition appears within a single demand and is available to the member reading it.

The papers before and after the reminder indicate how the secretary expected the information about collection to reach him. On 18 February a member was asked to bring the subscription on his next passage to the room, with a receipt to be given for the money received. On the 25th the secretary reported having distributed the term notices and wanted the treasurer to keep the receipts with the statement of accounts. Names for which a receipt was lacking were to be brought to his attention. The room could thus serve as a convenience for payment, and the collection of receipts as a means of finding accounts requiring further attention. Neither instruction describes an inspection of receipts at the entrance. [5]

On 6 March the secretary sent the treasurer a statement of the members whose subscriptions still had to be received and asked that amounts be written on their receipts. The reference to the previous month's notice connects the stated sum of one franc fifty centimes with the quarter in question. By 11 March the treasurer could say that he was returning the statement with the receipts received and had left blank the names whose accounts were still open. The accounts therefore had a way of recording unsettled items without entering a fictitious sum against them. The letter provides evidence of the return of receipts as a group, although it does not reproduce the statements by name or attach an identifiable receipt to the reminder of the 4th. [5]

The promise to give a receipt is significant in its own right. It identifies the subscription as a payment that could be acknowledged and subsequently checked, and it shows why an amount in a reminder belongs to the history of assessment. To recover one particular settlement would require the corresponding acknowledgment or an entry in the completed statement. The reason for the later blank also matters. Here the accountant tells the secretary what an open account looks like in the returned statement. A blank in a surviving copy cannot always be read by that rule. The treasurer's explanation belongs to his own return and is worth retaining with it.

Two years later a different set of papers described how envelopes moved from the room to the treasurer. On 9 February 1863 the treasurer told the attendant that members might leave envelopes at the room and asked him to keep them with the papers of the funds until collection. On the 12th the attendant reported that an envelope had arrived through a member and had been put with those papers. The person who sent the money, the member who brought the envelope, and the attendant who looked after it might each have been a different person. An address or signature can identify one of them without identifying the other two. [6]

The treasurer's letter of 14 February made that distinction explicit in a modest practical precaution. Because envelopes resembled each other, the attendant was to mark the sender's name and the amount on each, without substituting his own name. The papers were to remain closed until handed over. In such a system the person's visibility in the account depended on maintaining the information on the outside of an object as it passed through other hands. A name entered simply from the person handing over money in the room would have been an inadequate record even for the treasurer. Nor is the sequence sufficient to identify the unnamed member of the attendant's earlier letter as the sender in the next case. The later report of a single remittance for three members illustrates this accounting risk. Entering such a total under the attendant's name could leave individual shares entirely unrecorded on the stub kept at the cash desk. [2, 6]

The case of the coal gift on 16 February shows what the routing procedure was for. The treasurer had heard of an envelope left for the funds. Its label bore the sender and amount. He thanked the attendant for keeping it, asked him to bring the envelope containing two francs, and required the subscriptions received to be entered separately in the book. The two francs were given for coal. The treasurer expressly prohibited a confusion with subscriptions and proposed checking the entries at the cash desk. An envelope could thus enter an ordinary channel for the society's money while its contents remained assigned to a specific expense. [7]

The letter establishes that the envelope had been reported, and specifies the treasurer's intended handling of it. The attendant's next surviving letter says that he will bring the envelopes and the book. The treasurer then acknowledges receiving envelopes and speaks of comparing the entries. These are stronger traces of handling than a promise in the abstract, and they belong to the same short run of correspondence. The acknowledgment does not itemise the sums. It supports the movement towards settlement of a group of packets without supplying an individual posting for two francs. The separate accounting for the coal gift is established by the treasurer's instruction whether or not the corresponding book entry can be identified. [6]

The account had other ways to remain open. On 29 January 1866 the secretary wrote to the treasurer on behalf of the committee about the subscription still owed by a member. The expression *dispense jusqu'au 12 février* is followed by two directions. The treasurer is to withhold the demand before 12 February and resume the ordinary reminders after the 12th, « après le 12, reprenez les rappels ordinaires ». The renewed reminders concern the same outstanding subscription. The decision is a deferment of collection for a stated interval. A remission would have extinguished the sum and left the treasurer with no balance to demand under this instruction. [8]

A member's request for time occurs at the beginning of the January group, and the secretary informs a member of the committee's decision in the letter of 30 January. In early February the treasurer reports entering the decision on his statement. The treasurer's letter records the implementation of an administrative instruction. We can see precisely what the treasurer says he had done when he entered the decision. The instruction affords no figure for the debt. It gives no permission to use the room and states no suspension of such permission. The committee's communication with the treasurer therefore has a narrower subject than a general decision on the member's standing. [8]

The calendar in these papers gives the treasurer a way to retain the claim without treating it as immediately collectible. It is tempting to infer the experience of an indebted reader from that change. But a notice that specifies when to resume reminders is addressed to the holder of an account, and the secretary's notice to the member speaks about what demands he should expect. One can reconstruct an interval in the collection procedure from these papers. Any account of what the member did during the interval, or after the reminders were to resume, would need to follow another trace. The difference matters when placing these papers beside the separate stove offer later that February.

These instances give the assessment and collection correspondence its comparative value. The reminder articulates the due amount and the recipient of payment. The envelope instructions make money and the information about it jointly transferable. The temporary deferment shows that the account could be managed through time and that such management could itself be entered on a statement. Each operation preserves the distinction between the subscription and other money. That distinction will matter again at a reading for which heating was supported by an offering and in a case where holding money for the room ended by giving some of it back.

## §3 Invitations and gifts

The Saturday invitation of 28 October 1862 opens with a meeting at the usual hour in the society's room. The description presupposes an audience that knows the hour and a place already in use. Each member might bring one companion. Members and guests might make an *offrande libre* for heating. The secretary follows that clause with « Nous accueillerons aussi ceux qui ne donneront rien », “We shall also welcome those who give nothing” (f. 085r, l. 5). The next line asks members to present their companions to the attendant at the entrance. The one-companion allowance sets the scope of the invitation, the introduction gives the attendant a way to recognise guests, and the explicit welcome extends to those who make no contribution. [9]

The guest's relation to a member is the practical unit of this arrangement. A companion may be someone whom the attendant has never met. Presentation at the door allows the attendant to act on the invitation without having to treat each stranger as entitled to enter. In turn, one companion per member puts a limit on what any member can claim under this particular invitation. It gives no count of how many invitations were taken up. Nor does the invitation tell us how to name a companion independently if the name was omitted from a later record. The procedure expects a connection between people to be made at the entrance. The paper is addressed to the people who can make it.

The word *libre* in *offrande libre* attaches to the offering. It would be easy to overlook its consequence if the notice were summarised as requiring a payment at the door. There is an entrance in this letter but the instruction involving the entrance concerns presenting a person. The notice treats the offering for heating as voluntary, a provision which gives neither a minimum sum nor an obligation to pay. I have therefore read the companion provision as part of the invitation, and the offering as a contribution that could be made by the people invited. The proximity of the two provisions is a reason to read them with some care. [9]

The short letters before the invitation help to recover the work that made it practicable. On 25 October the seating instructions asked that a clear passage be left from the door to the back of the room. On the 27th the writer had prepared the addresses and proposed making two packets, one of letters for members of the neighbourhood and another for the rest, subject to approval before the envelopes were closed. It is possible to see a distinction among the recipients at the stage of distribution without attaching a street or route to either packet. The record of preparations conveys considerable attention to the circulation of an invitation and relatively little about its reception. [10]

The 30 October letter to the attendant repeats the possibility that a member will present the person accompanying him and tells the attendant to conduct arrivals to the seats. This is the companion provision expressed as work to be done by someone other than the member. The attendant's place near the entrance and the prepared seats give two successive points in the instructions. They add substance to the undertaking in the invitation without constituting its fulfilment. If an attendee subsequently wrote about the reading, the seating instructions might help in reconstructing a particular experience. As they stand, the instructions show arrangements for reception, writing in advance of it. [10]

The issue extends beyond the definition of a single gift. In the subscription notice of 1861 the secretary could demand a payment from a member and distinguish it from offerings for a lamp. The Saturday notice addresses an audience that includes people who hold no such position in the account described there. By extending the language of giving to members and guests, it admits a source of support that does not depend on having a subscription to settle. Equally, it does not give the contributor a standing claim to return to the next event. The letter is framed around one reading. Its description of a way of being welcomed and a way of giving should both retain that occasion.

The coal envelope from 1863 gives a second means of putting money towards the room, without the form of an invitation. It is already reported as deposited, the sum is named, and the treasurer directs its passage through the attendant's hands. No invitation to a reading belongs to that exchange. What it shares with the heating clause is a purpose that could be understood without treating the funds as subscriptions. It does not follow that all letters mentioning fuel describe the same practice. The Saturday notice makes an offer of a way to give to an audience. The coal letter tells a custodian what to do with money sent. [7]

A letter of 15 February 1866 supplies the member's side of such a distinction. He had yet to settle his subscription and did not give a date at which he would pay it. Meanwhile he proposed one franc for the stove. He expressly asked the secretary to receive it for that purpose, without entering it against the overdue subscription, and meant to bring it to the room on Friday. The juxtaposition makes it impossible to explain the proposed franc as an instalment simply because a recognised debtor offered it. The member is the person making the distinction, and he asks the secretary to preserve it in receiving the money. [11]

The member expected to bring the franc on Friday. That intention establishes the proposed place and occasion of delivery. A dated entry for a gift received would require a separate acknowledgment. On 17 February the secretary thanked the member for his letter and said that he would put the offer before the committee. He also said he would write again after its next meeting. The reply acknowledges receipt of the proposal and sets out the intended next step. The papers leave open whether the offer reached the committee, whether it was accepted and whether the member brought the franc to the room on the Friday he had proposed. A subsequent letter could establish the outcome. [11]

The January deferment and the February stove offer are close in date and both occur among arrears papers. The similarity should stop short of connecting their unnamed members. One document communicates a committee decision to a treasurer, the other records a member asking a secretary to receive a gift. In the first case we can follow the temporary suspension of a demand. In the second we can follow a proposal for an expense, expressly insulated from an existing demand. Nothing in the surviving passages identifies the members with one another. Reading them side by side establishes that different forms of discretion existed within the society's dealings with money still due. [8, 11]

The next meeting gives the secretary a point after which to answer. It leaves the offered franc in suspense and supplies no date at which the member's separate subscription would be settled. [11]

The Thursday lamp papers give a further view of money held for an expense. In 1868 the attendant reports having received one franc fifty centimes for the lamp and having taken it to the treasurer with papers. The equal amount to the quarterly subscription in the earlier letter is coincidental as far as these records show. Here the money is to be held separately until the expense is known. After the expense has been settled, the attendant asks for the remaining seventy-five centimes so that he can return them to the donor. The direction that the remainder does not go to subscriptions makes the distinction explicit even when the original purpose no longer requires the full sum. [12]

The return runs from treasurer through attendant to giver. Reading only the words asking for seventy-five centimes back might suggest a rebate. Reading the preceding report of transfer and the subsequent instruction about its destination fixes the direction and the category of the money. On 15 October the treasurer wrote that he had put the lamp balance with the attendant's book, apart from the subscription accounts. That statement advances the return by one stage. It leaves open the moment at which the giver actually received the change. The sequence includes money already handled, a request for a further transfer and a report that the balance was made available. [12]

Keeping the whole sequence visible prevents the event from becoming an entry for negative dues. It also keeps the economy of the occasion distinct from the count of readers. The lighting expense belonged to the operation of the room, which explains the reason for accepting and holding funds for that purpose. It need not tell us whether the donor came on Thursday or what the light enabled any particular reader to do. The gift had a history extending before and after the opening of the room and passing through people whose own visits for the purpose of reading are not in evidence in these letters.

Taken together, these passages show the advantage of following an offering to the particular task that called it forth. The heating clause sits within preparations for inviting people. The stove offer expresses a member's initiative apart from his subscription. The lamp balance belongs to the attendant's settlement of a specific expense. The most informative resemblance among them is the preservation of purpose as money is invited, promised or put into other hands. In none of the three would the historian improve the evidence by treating a payment mentioned in writing as an admission ticket. An invitation's terms and a facility's upkeep are both parts of the history of reading.

## §4 Reading in the room and borrowing

The March 1866 correspondence in box 14 approaches reading through an apparently small change of place. A book is in the room, and a member writes to ask whether he can take it home to read. On 19 March the secretary tells the attendant that the member's account still shows an unpaid subscription. He then permits continued consultation in the room and directs the attendant to leave the books needed for reading on the premises at the member's disposal (f. 186r, ll. 3–4). Lines 5–9 turn to what the attendant is to do if the member asks to take a book away. Payment of the arrears is due before the attendant releases the book. The two instructions specify different terms for consultation on the premises and for borrowing. [13]

In the instruction the person who has to act is the attendant. If a request to remove a book arrives, he is to receive the amount still due before letting the book go and then give that amount with the member's name to the treasurer. The money is the existing subscription. The letter gives no new charge to be levied for each book and specifies no price for consulting a book at a table. The secretary's identification of an already open account explains why the attendant needs to transmit the name. Without it the treasurer would have the money but would lack the information by which to apply it. [13]

The condition takes effect at the boundary of what the attendant is authorised to do with the book. That is a narrower claim than one about the right of an indebted person to enter under every circumstance. The secretary's concern is the outstanding subscription in the event of a loan. The distinction between on-site consultation and carrying a book away should be retained in translation. Otherwise the instruction could become an explanation of what all readers had to pay at the door, an explanation that would put the wrong person, place and occasion into the financial exchange. The arrears follow the member's account even when the relevant question arises over a particular book. The February instruction for deferred collection also required the attendant to refer any requests to take books away to the secretary while the deferment remained in effect. [14]

It is worth comparing the direction to send the money with the member's name with the instruction for envelopes in box 12. Both instructions separate the keeper or bearer of money from the person to whom the sum belongs for accounting purposes. The envelope could contain money for several kinds of purpose, so its label preserves information supplied by or about the sender. In the borrowing case the secretary identifies both the kind of money and the person whose arrears are to be settled. One procedure begins with an object already deposited, the other with a possible request. The care about names shared by the two procedures does not turn them into observations of the same thing. [6, 13]

There is also something to be learned from the location of the papers. Because this instruction is filed among arrears, one could use it only as another example of a secretary trying to secure payment. Yet the occurrence of the demand depends on a contemplated use of a book, and the text identifies a person who can stop that book leaving. Conversely, a study of borrowing restricted to a heading for loans would miss the relevance of the account. The useful connection between money and books here is an institutional one, established by the instruction. Its effect on the decision whether to borrow remains a separate question for which the letter supplies no answer.

The adjacent papers allow one to see some of the distance between a request and a loan. On 15 March a member had asked whether he might take an ouvrage home and said he would ask the attendant for the reply. On 22 March the attendant said he had left the work in the room and that the member had asked about the answer. That statement reports an encounter and a book kept available in the room. It does not say that the work was then carried out. The 26 March letter thanks the secretary for a reply and expresses an intention to come and look at some pages again. The letter's partly illegible conclusion cannot certify what happened to the book. [13]

The encounter reported by the attendant gives one more definite piece of evidence than a permission by itself. It records a question put to him. It would still take a step beyond the words to turn the question into a loan or into a particular period of reading. A book left for reading supplies an opportunity, while the statement that someone asked for a decision describes a completed action. Even where the documents read naturally in succession, the kind of action established by each survives their arrangement as a sequence. Their order lets us see a procedure in progress without filling in its result.

A brief report returned with an evening register in box 13 reaches further into actual use. On 5 December 1865 an attendant explained that one person had requested a newspaper and then a book before closing. He had put the requests on different lines to distinguish the things supplied. He asked the secretary to count a single visit, because the reader remained in the room between requests. The letter also set aside another line on which a name was difficult to read. The writer intended to check it before associating it with another name. Both remarks restrict what a compiler could safely derive from the register. [3]

This report assigns a meaning to a pair of lines that could easily be missed by a reader who had the entries alone. The unit of inscription is a request or an object as handled by the attendant. The unit he proposes for counting arrivals is a visit. One may recover an instance of use because he explains the relation between the two in writing, and that explanation also makes clear why two marks need not represent two people. Neither the explanatory letter nor the two requests tells us the state of the reader's subscription. The uncertainty over the other name is another reason to keep the link between an observation and its named subject under examination.

The interest of the report for the March condition is therefore indirect. It gives an example of the kind of notice of actual use which the secretary's conditional instruction cannot provide. It belongs to an evening in the previous December, and the two requests do not include an account of a loan outside the premises. It would be wrong to give them the borrowing instruction's meaning simply because the second request was for a book. An attendant can handle and record an object in the room. The secretary's instruction concerns the additional event of its removal. A book is present in both descriptions, yet the actions and the grounds for knowing them differ.

A smaller act of opening can be established elsewhere. On 16 October 1868 an attendant reported that the room had opened on Thursday at the ordinary hour, that he had retrieved the key and put away the lamp after the reading, and that he had left the book on the table for the secretary. This retrospective account shows that the arrangements resulted in an opening and that the objects used for the duty were put away. It tells us little about attendance. Its documentary advantage over a notice of opening hours is confined to the action reported. It answers whether the room opened that evening more directly than a proposed timetable could. [15]

Putting the retrospective report against the prospective key-box notices shows how evidence becomes more or less secure at different points of a claim. The notices give a particular attendant a time and a way to obtain keys. The report for the October evening establishes a completed opening on that occasion. It does not establish performance of a notice for a later date. One may compare the types of labour in both without manufacturing a continuous record of evenings. The same restriction keeps the March exchange within its own dates, even when its account of reading leaves a question which the December report might appear to answer. [15, 16]

This is also where a distinction between participation in the conduct of the society and participation as a reader does some work. The attendant who opens the room, the officer who takes the receipts and the donor who pays for a lamp each makes an activity possible. Some of those people may also be readers, and a single evening might bring the roles together. The documentary fact is the action for which the person appears in that passage. Converting it into an instance of reading requires evidence that the paper itself may have had no occasion to preserve. A study of participation can acknowledge the first kind of involvement while treating the second as a proposition to be checked.

Under the March instruction a proposed removal of a book gave the attendant a specific occasion to collect arrears. Its usefulness is to identify the point at which the attendant was to collect and the purpose of his handover to the treasurer. The readier evidence of actual use belongs to those records which recount an individual request or an evening's handling. Placing the two kinds of material against one another tells us more about the opportunities and constraints of reading than either a list of amounts or a tally of book references could. It leaves the relative frequency of kinds of reading to documents capable of supplying it.

## §5 Reading across places

Place-names enter these papers because someone has to act away from the writer. The person paying needs to know where the treasurer can receive a sum. The attendant who will open a room needs to find the keys. Members meeting for a departure need a place at which to assemble. To the writers, a name may be adequate together with an hour and a familiar mark. To a reader much later, the same shorthand may invite a geographical interpretation stronger than the passage warrants. Its first value is as part of the instruction. The words around the name tell us the part that a place was to play in an arrangement.

The January packet was small enough for Stefan Albrecht and me to work through it together in leaf order. There were two pairs of leaves for each of boxes 12, 13 and 14. We marked twelve place-name occurrences in the first box, fourteen in the second and twenty in the third. These totals concern the spellings at specific points on the leaves. A name may occur again on a different face of the same leaf, or two names can occur in one notice and perform different functions. There are forty-six occurrences in all. The unit here can be checked by turning back to the reference and seeing where the name is used. [17]

An initial attempt to strip articles, accents and hyphens from the names gave a poor guide to the comparisons that mattered. A separated form and a hyphenated one can remain recognisable even without a computer's proposed key. But *Clairpré* and *Clerpré* diverge in more than the accent. Conversely, the common noun *clos* can bring together phrases whose apparent resemblance is of no use in identifying a receiving point. The purpose of an alphabetical arrangement is to make the papers accessible to people looking for them. An arrangement which suppresses the form a reader sees on the page introduces a needless difficulty at the first step. [17]

The notices of 4 and 6 November 1862 furnish a stronger basis for bringing two spellings together. The earlier notice asks members to assemble at ten at Clairpré near the stone bridge (CR/13/A f. 089r, ll. 2–3). The later notice opens « L'avis du 4 novembre demande une correction d'heure » (f. 090r, l. 1). It keeps the stone bridge as the meeting point, writes *Clerpré* and substitutes eleven for ten (ll. 2–4). Its last line expressly leaves the other arrangements in force. The dated reference and the restriction of the correction to the hour justify filing both occurrences under Clairpré, with their spellings and references intact. In the first notice *Bas-Maret* names the point from which a delivery is expected (f. 089r, l. 7). The wording distinguishes that point of departure from the assembly at the bridge. [18]

The difference between the two names in the earlier notice is comparable to the difference between a sender and a carrier in the collection papers. The document puts the two places into a relation for a purpose. Its direction and wording matter more than their proximity on lines of type. A register that extracted both names and discarded the surrounding verbs would make the relation harder to see. Reading the notice as an instruction preserves the expected delivery as well as the arrangement for assembly. It also keeps the apparent change of spelling in the later notice from looking like the record of a new gathering somewhere else.

Box 12 offers two particularly spare indications of place. The payment notice of 3 June 1861 specifies a morning at *le Pré des Ormes*, where members who have their last receipts can present themselves. The secretary's language gives the landmark a role in reception on that date. The treasurer's instruction of 16 April 1863 tells the collector to give an envelope carrying a statement to the keeper at *les Hautes Bornes*. The cloth-covered book stays with the treasurer. The envelope has to move and the keeper is to acknowledge receiving it. Neither the term for payment nor the instruction about the envelope supplies a district to which the landmark can securely be assigned. [19]

The treasurer's instruction about the keeper names two distinct tasks. The collector is to deliver the envelope to the keeper at the landmark. The keeper's receipt is then to go back to the treasurer. The account book remains with him throughout this projected exchange. Such a sequence shows why a place register must preserve what each destination is doing in a sentence. In the instructions about the service book, the agreed meeting at la Combe aux Joncs is for a check of the keys. The book is to stay with the incoming attendant until the next turn. These are two stages of responsibility, and the meeting supplies the place for only one of them. [19]

Consider next the November 1868 key-box notices. The first asks an attendant to leave the box at Brévalette on Thursday evening and expects the following attendant to request it before opening. The second announces a temporary collection point, *le Clos Nerlin*, and corrects the opening hour for the next sitting from six to seven. The time and means of taking possession of the keys are both germane to the work of an attendant. No address is given for either collection point. The box itself is the same sort of object in successive arrangements, which makes the comparison possible. The two passages do not give the grounds for identifying the temporary point with the earlier deposit. [16]

A deposit after a sitting and a collection before the next opening describe different duties, even when each involves a box of keys. The two November notices allow a comparison of those duties on the strength of what the instructions actually specify. One tells the outgoing attendant where to leave the box. The other tells the incoming attendant where to appear before opening on the occasion for which the hour is changed. Treating the curiously specific landmarks as points on a route would add an itinerary that the two notices leave entirely to the people doing the work. Even the hour of opening gives us only a deadline for obtaining the keys beforehand. [16]

The folded instruction on leaf 176 recto shows the difference between settling a reading and settling a location. In the address line (CR/14/A f. 176r, l. 4) the first letter lies at a fold. The movement of the pen in the V of *Vu* (l. 2) helps to distinguish the apparent initial from the B of *Berger* (l. 8). The latter has two swellings where the proposed V has a turn. The surviving strokes favour *Val d’Erne*, with the turn at the fold still open to inspection. The leaf allows us to read an address on a forwarding copy. It lacks a contemporary address endorsement or description that would locate that address, and gives no identifiable relay for the notice. Berger is named in connection with another packet. I leave the address off the map and the route undrawn. A geographical identification would require a contemporary account of the location, and a route would also require evidence of the intervening handovers. [20]

There is a related uncertainty in the treasurer's letter of 22 February. He says he will leave an envelope at *la Côte aux Ifs* so that it can be picked up and asks the secretary to note the handover before filing the statement. The enclosure is distinguished from the book of payments. This is enough to know how the place figures in the writer's account of the envelope. The letter gives no onward destination, and nothing in it connects the handover to the notice addressed to Val d’Erne. Likewise the rendezvous at *la Combe aux Joncs* for the service papers and keys is given in terms intelligible to the addressee, with no address for the person bringing the papers. [19, 20]

Thirty-eight of the forty-six occurrences could be assigned to headings in the January exercise. Eight remain independent. The figures count passages examined. The same place can account for several occurrences, and a legible name can still be unsatisfactory as an address. The count checks that no occurrence was lost in matching the spellings. [17]

Keeping the source reference with the spelling makes later corrections possible. If a fuller address comes to light or the initial on a leaf is read differently, the reader can return to the passage and record the grounds for a new heading while retaining the original occurrence.

For a comparison of societies the gain is more modest and more tangible. A reference can be made stable enough for another reader to recover the terms on which people were invited to assemble or expected to convey something. At that point the passages can be put beside arrangements elsewhere on a common footing. A condition of reception at a named point remains a condition, whether it can be placed precisely or only followed back to a line. I have consequently used place in this chapter to explain the organisation of particular acts. Whether people then moved between inferred points, and in what numbers, is left open.

## §6 Limits of the comparison

The first question of the chapter concerned the relation between money due and the use of a reading society. The sources allow us to put the relation in more exact terms than a comparison between sums called fees. The secretary could insist on the quarterly subscription and set an offering outside that account. The committee could give a member time while retaining a claim. A member could support the stove while acknowledging his outstanding subscription. The separate operations matter because a description of how the society paid to maintain the room is only one part of a description of what members and their companions could do in it.

A second question concerned what happens when written arrangements are read as though they were a record of everyone who took part. The invitation describes who might be brought to one reading. The loan instruction places a condition on the removal of a book. The February treasurer's acknowledgment is about envelopes he had received. One could write a defensible history of the expectation that members support the society and of the different means by which they or others could do so. A total assembled from these traces would constantly change its meaning as the historian moved between invitations, demands, replies and records of handling.

There are cases in which local evidence enables us to go further. The treasurer reports entering a decision on his statement. An attendant recounts opening the room and putting the lamp away. The evening attendant explains how many visits two successive requests represented. These passages establish particular actions on the writers' testimony. Their strength does not derive from the amount of information they contain. A brief past-tense acknowledgment may have more bearing on an act than several pages setting out instructions. Its strength comes from the connection the writer makes between what was done and the particular sum, object or occasion he had reason to record.

There is no basis here for comparing visit rates for members whose subscriptions were paid and members in arrears. For a defined period I would need an independent record of actual visits and a way to link each visitor to the dated subscription status of the same member. I would also need the number of eligible members in each payment group, including those who made no visit, to supply the denominators. The available papers supply neither a linkable series of visits for a common period nor those denominators. The December attendant distinguishes one visit from two requests, but does not give the reader's payment status. The March instruction gives a condition of borrowing for an indebted member. It supplies no series of dated arrivals. Restricting the comparison to legible names in both kinds of record would leave their coverage and the eligible populations unknown. [3, 13]

The history of preparation is more accessible than that comparison of frequency. The willingness to hold a sum apart for the Thursday lamp or arrange an individual delay can be described with some confidence. Both actions show how a collective activity could accommodate different financial purposes at once. The attendant who had to keep the labels in order and the officer who had to convey the committee's decision were doing work on which the stated arrangements depended. Whether the arrangement would produce more visits is a further question. The surviving examples are spaced over several years and were chosen for the words or acts they could clarify.

The same difficulty applies to using the January place register to estimate movement. An identified heading makes it easier to retrieve multiple passages about an instruction. It does not tell us how many people went there, whether a proposed collection took place, or whether an object was carried onward by the person first authorised to collect it. A single meeting place can recur across notices even when the directions tell us nothing about the paths that different members might have taken to reach it. Very little about the reach of the society can be inferred from the frequency with which a name appears in a set of documents copied for a particular enquiry. [17]

Nor does the uneven survival of the papers favour a precise negative conclusion. A charge or an exception that we do not find in a group of leaves may have been recorded elsewhere. That possibility does not authorise us to put the conjectured exception into the account. It does remind us to give the date and provenance of evidence for a condition, especially when moving from one isolated instruction to a description of a habit. The opening arrangements can be compared with a later report if its occasion is stated. A gap between them remains a gap in what we can say about actual practice.

The comparison therefore makes its clearest contribution at the point where a condition reaches someone who has to interpret or apply it. A rule or a dated instruction issued by the responsible officer establishes the terms in force for the people and occasion it covers. The Saturday notice supplies the companion allowance, and the secretary's March letter supplies the terms for consulting and taking away books in the case before him. An act of collection calls for a receipt, a completed account entry or an acknowledgment identifying the sum actually received. The acknowledgment of the envelopes establishes receipt at the level it describes. A claim about actual use calls for a record of what a reader did, such as an attendant's account of an arrival and requests, or an entry recording a completed loan. The December report establishes one visit through the attendant's explanation of two requests. Keeping the authority for a condition, evidence of its application and testimony of use separate makes the terms of participation comparable across the papers while leaving the incidence of attendance open.
