# Chapter 1: Scope and sources

LHT-20250626-CHP-001 | 10 July 2025 | Judith Morris | v0.2  
For Roger Hewitt

## 1. The body and its officers

The society at Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive was established with the approval of the town.⁵⁵ Its working arrangements are more easily followed through the papers of its officers than through the formal record of its foundation. The secretary's circular of 4 February 1861 tells members where to send the subscription and assigns the entry of each remittance to the treasurer. The treasurer keeps the receipt counterfoils and the secretary files the statement supplied by the treasurer.¹ The continuation asks anyone remitting for several people to identify the members covered by the payment.² Even within this short notice, money, the receipt and the statement take different routes.

The secretary's notice of 3 June sets the morning of 10 June for receiving payments at le Pré des Ormes. On the back, the attendant is told to send the duplicate receipts to the secretary at Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive. The treasurer is to send the cash separately.³ In the reply of 11 June, the secretary acknowledges the duplicates and asks that a blank one be struck from the statement if it was counted among receipts actually issued.⁴ The place of collection and the address for returning paperwork serve different purposes. For comparison with other reading societies, I begin with the duties that a document assigns to each office and with the places at which those duties were to be carried out.

## 2. Regulations and collection

The subscription regulations took effect from the day they were posted.⁵⁶ The February circular sets a quarterly subscription of one franc fifty centimes, with the first payment due by 15 February.¹ In the later February papers, a member is asked to pay the treasurer the next time he comes to the room, and the treasurer is asked to retain the receipts with the statement and report any missing receipt by name.⁵ ⁶ In March, a reminder separates the overdue subscription from the gift for the lamp. The secretary sends the treasurer a list of unpaid subscriptions and asks for a completed statement in return.⁷ ⁸ When the treasurer sends it back, he reports leaving the names of members with open accounts blank and refers to a separate sheet for the lamp money. The reading of the word for 'separate' on that leaf is less certain than the distinction in the earlier instructions.⁹ The names of those in arrears were displayed at the entrance.⁵⁷

The 1863 correspondence shows what can happen between a request for payment and an entry in the accounts. Members could leave envelopes in the room for the treasurer, and the attendant reported holding one with the cash papers.¹⁰ ¹¹ The treasurer asked for the sender's name and the sum on each closed envelope.¹² A subsequent letter identifies two francs as a gift for coal and directs that subscriptions be entered separately in the notebook.¹³ The attendant then writes that he will bring the labelled envelopes and leave the notebook for checking. The treasurer acknowledges receipt of the envelopes and says he will return the notebook after comparing the entries.¹⁴ ¹⁵

On 2 March the treasurer reports that he received one remittance the previous day on behalf of three members. His practice of entering the total under the intermediary's name left the amount attributable to each account uncertain. The letter asks how to keep the details on the counterfoil and the statement. It gives no separate entry for the intermediary's own subscription.¹⁶ The April papers follow the receipt for a different kind of handover. On 15 April the treasurer asks for the receipt to be returned and filed with the April remittances, with the statement of sums to remain provisional until then.¹⁷ On 16 April he directs the attendant to put the collection statement in an envelope with the supporting papers and hand it to the custodian at les Hautes Bornes. On 17 April the attendant records returning the custodian's receipt for that envelope to the treasurer. The endorsement records that the receipt was slipped into the remittances leaf.¹⁸

## 3. Guests and records of use

The October papers follow the preparations for a Saturday reading. The secretary asks the attendant to arrange chairs and leave a clear passage from the entrance.¹⁹ On 27 October the attendant tells the secretary that he has prepared the addresses. He proposes putting letters for members of the district in one bundle and the rest in another, and asks whether this division is suitable before sealing the envelopes.²⁰ The secretary's invitation of 28 October permits each member to bring one companion and makes the contribution towards heating voluntary.²¹ The duty instruction of 30 October stations the attendant at the entrance to receive companions presented by members and show the arrivals to their seats.²²

The invitation for 8 November 1862 likewise allows a member to bring a companion. It specifies that the invitation is for that sitting and confers no membership.²³ The postscript tells the attendant to list the guests separately and to put the presenting member's name beside each guest.²⁴ The guests were subsequently registered as members.⁵⁸ If one follows the names from the door into the membership record, the Saturday invitation belongs in an account of recruitment as well as in an account of the reading.

The notice of 4 November asks members to meet by the stone bridge at Clairpré and says that the courier from Bas-Maret is expected there.²⁵ The correction of 6 November moves the rendezvous from ten to eleven while keeping the bridge as the meeting place.²⁶ The delivery from the meeting place formed part of the branch's activity.⁵⁹

A different problem arises in putting visits into numbers. On 5 December 1865 the evening attendant explains that he entered a newspaper and then a book on separate lines because the objects differed. The reader stayed in the room throughout, and the attendant asks the secretary to count only one visit.²⁷ The service papers of 7–9 January that year document the return and transfer of a notebook and keys, with a rendezvous at la Combe aux Joncs for the incoming attendant. They concern the custody of the record.²⁸ ²⁹ Evening opening was a regular public day.⁶⁰ A comparison of evening provision will still need to keep the occasions on which the room was available separate from the requests entered in the notebook.

## 4. Deferment and permission

A January letter asks for more time to pay. Another letter concerns the keeping of the room's papers for the committee.³⁰ ³¹ On 29 January 1866 the secretary tells the treasurer that the committee has granted a delay until 12 February, after which the ordinary reminders are to resume. The secretary writes to the member the next day, and the treasurer reports entering the decision on the statement on 3 February.³² ³³ ³⁴ Two other papers show decisions passing through the collection office. One directs delivery of a closed notice to Val d’Erne and retention of the duplicate. In the other, the treasurer arranges to leave an envelope with the statement of deferments at la Côte aux Ifs.³⁵ ³⁶

In the case communicated on 9 February, the committee defers collection until the end of February and the secretary tells the attendant to let the person continue reading in the room. A request to take a volume away is to be recorded and referred to the secretary before the book is handed over.³⁷ The attendant is asked to retain the notice until the end of the month and report difficulties.³⁸ An exemption from subscription also carried permission to borrow books outside the room.⁶¹

The exchanges about the stove illustrate another distinction. The secretary writes about the needs of the room, a member whose subscription is unpaid offers one franc for the stove and asks for it to be kept off his subscription account, and the secretary promises to put the offer before the committee.³⁹ ⁴⁰ ⁴¹ The March correspondence gives a separate example of a member asking to take a book home. In the secretary's instructions of 19 March the person concerned may continue to read on the premises, with the arrears to be paid before a book goes out. The attendant subsequently reports leaving the book at the room, and a letter of 26 March expresses an intention to come and consult the pages again.⁴² ⁴³ ⁴⁴ ⁴⁵ The successive papers give us requests and conditions in more detail than they give us the outcome of a loan.

## 5. Hours of opening

On 6 October 1868 the committee instructs the Thursday attendants to try closing at eight instead of seven in the evening during October. It provides for the transfer of the register and keys at seven and assigns the lighting to the attendant on the extended shift.⁴⁶ The continuation asks for arrivals between seven and eight to be entered separately, without counting anyone already present at seven a second time. The journal is to be examined at the end of the month.⁴⁷ The room remained open until eight on Thursdays throughout the winter.⁶² The committee extended the October trial.⁶³

The attendant's and treasurer's papers show the work that keeping a room open entailed. One attendant leaves the key and notebook for his successor and points out where the lamp is kept.⁴⁸ On 12 October the attendant reports taking a member's gift of one franc fifty centimes for the Thursday lamp to the treasurer. He asks the treasurer to keep the sum apart until the expense is known.⁴⁹ On 13 October, with the lamp expense settled, he asks for the remaining seventy-five centimes back so that he can return them to the giver.⁵⁰ On 15 October the treasurer says that he has placed the balance with the attendant's notebook, separate from the subscription accounts, for collection at the Thursday duty.⁵¹ The correspondence takes the balance as far as the notebook. On 16 October the attendant reports opening on Thursday at the customary hour and putting away the lamp after the reading.⁵²

The November papers locate the key box at Brévalette for the next attendant and name le Clos Nerlin as the temporary place to collect the keys for a particular sitting. The second notice also corrects the announced opening from six to seven.⁵³ ⁵⁴ These instructions are useful for distinguishing the time at which an attendant must obtain access from the time at which a reader may enter. A deposit for the keys need not be at the room itself.

## 6. What survives

The catalogue of the Archives de Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive gives 235 numbered positions across boxes 12–14. Of these, 216 are available for copying and 19 are recorded as missing. Box 12 accounts for 72 available leaves and six missing positions, box 13 for 68 and five, and box 14 for 76 and eight. The papers on my desk comprise ten leaves from the first visit, thirty-two from the September consignment and twelve received in January. The fifty-four leaves therefore cover only part of the catalogue. Even within the surviving ranges, neighbouring folios can belong to different exchanges or record successive stages of the same transaction.

For the chapters that follow, I shall keep a distinction between a rule circulated to members, an instruction to an officer and the record of an act carried out. The comparisons concern the terms under which people could use a room and the work that made such use possible. A payment, a guest's name and a request for a book count different things. Estimates of the people who came will depend on finding records that identify visits consistently.
