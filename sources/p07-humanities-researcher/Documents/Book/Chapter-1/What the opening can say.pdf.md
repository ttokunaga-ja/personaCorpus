# What the opening can say

LHT-20250722-PAP-001 · 22 July 2025 · Judith Morris · v1.0  
For Roger Hewitt

## 1. Begin with the work of the officers

The notice of 4 February 1861 gives us a way to describe who ran the room's affairs. The secretary asks members to pay one franc fifty centimes for the quarter and directs the treasurer to enter each remittance and retain the counterfoil. The secretary keeps the statement that the treasurer sends him. The continuation asks a person sending money for several members to name the members covered. We can compare how societies divided the receipt of money, the keeping of accounts and the custody of papers. The notice leaves the question of who authorised the society's establishment open.¹

## 2. Keep invitations and use in separate accounts

For the sitting on 8 November 1862, each member could bring one companion. The invitation expressly limits this permission to the sitting. In the postscript the attendant is told to keep a separate guest list, naming the presenting member beside each guest and leaving the guests out of the membership count. This gives us a condition of entry and an instruction for recording it. A dated entry in the admission register would be needed before we could discuss a particular guest becoming a member.²

The evening attendant's letter of 5 December 1865 does report an instance of use. He entered the newspaper and the book on two lines because they were different objects. The same reader remained in the room between the two requests, so the attendant asks the secretary to count one visit. We can say how this pair of entries should be read. To count visits across the register, we would need to see how other attendants dealt with repeated requests on other evenings.³

## 3. Put a date and a limit on permissions

On 9 February 1866 the secretary conveyed a particular deferment of collection to the end of February and instructed the attendant to allow reading on the premises in the meantime. A request to take a volume away had to be written down and referred to the secretary before any handover. The notice gives the person permission to continue reading on site during the deferment and describes a separate procedure for a loan. For an actual visit we need a record of use, such as the attendant's December letter.⁴

On 6 October 1868 the committee instructed the Thursday attendants to try closing at eight instead of seven in the evening during October. The continuation assigns a separate part of the journal to arrivals in the extra hour and says to leave out anybody already present at seven. The committee proposed inspecting that journal at the end of the month before deciding whether to keep the hour. The instruction therefore supplies the terms of an October trial. Evidence of winter opening would have to come from dated winter papers.⁵

## 4. Set the comparison at the level of the evidence

In comparing provincial reading societies, I would ask who handled money and records, who could enter on a stated occasion and what an entry actually counted. I would compare a rule with a rule, a dated permission with another permission, and an account of a visit with a record that identifies a visit. Differences in accounting and in the survival of the papers matter before any comparison of how often people came. The nine claims set aside on 10 July can stay in the enquiry notes while the opening proceeds from what we have been able to read.
