# Chapter 1: Scope and sources

LHT-20250626-CHP-001 | 21 July 2025 | Judith Morris | v0.3  
For Roger Hewitt

## 1. The body and its officers

At Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive the working arrangements of the reading society come into view through the papers of its officers. In his circular of 4 February 1861 the secretary directs members to send their subscription to the treasurer, either themselves or through another member. On the first leaf the treasurer is told to enter each remittance on his receipts statement and keep the counterfoil with the cash papers. The secretary is to keep the statement that the treasurer supplies.¹ At the top of the next leaf the person remitting for several people is asked to identify those whose subscriptions the payment covers.² The records allow us to describe the duties of the two officers and the part a member could play in forwarding the money.

The collection notice of 3 June 1861 supplies a second example of that division of labour. It appoints the morning of 10 June for receiving payments at le Pré des Ormes. On the back the attendant is instructed to forward the duplicate receipts to the secretary at Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive, while the treasurer is to send the cash separately.³ On 11 June the secretary acknowledges the duplicates, returns a blank one and asks the treasurer to strike it from the statement if it was counted among the receipts issued.⁴ The address at which papers were received and the place set for collection belong to different parts of the transaction. The comparison starts with the offices and their recorded duties, and distinguishes a place used for one of those duties from the premises in which readers were received.

## 2. Regulations and collection

The February circular gives the quarterly subscription as one franc fifty centimes and sets 15 February as the first payment deadline.¹ A subsequent request asks a member to pay the treasurer on his next visit and provides for a receipt.⁵ The secretary reports that the notices have gone out and tells the treasurer to keep the receipts with the statement and give the names for which a receipt is missing.⁶ The March reminder treats the unpaid quarterly subscription and an offering for the lamp as separate sums.⁷ The secretary sends the treasurer the list of outstanding subscriptions and asks for the completed statement back, with the lamp gifts separately entered.⁸ On returning the statement the treasurer reports leaving blank the names whose accounts are still open. My reading of séparée for the lamp sheet on that leaf is tentative. The earlier instructions give the clearer evidence for how gifts were to be treated.⁹

A second set of papers follows money from the room towards the accounts. The treasurer allows members to leave envelopes with the attendant, who reports holding one with the cash papers.¹⁰ ¹¹ Each envelope is to remain closed and bear the amount and the sender's name.¹² In one letter the treasurer identifies two francs as a gift for coal and directs the attendant to make separate entries for subscriptions.¹³ The attendant reports that the envelopes are marked and proposes bringing them and leaving the notebook for comparison. The treasurer acknowledges the envelopes and says he will return the notebook after checking it.¹⁴ ¹⁵ The letters trace an authorised route for payments and, in the acknowledgement, a particular handover.

On 2 March 1863 the treasurer reports a single remittance received the day before on behalf of three members. He explains his practice of entering the total under the duty attendant's name when the attendant brought the money. He asks where to put the bearer's name and the individual shares, on the counterfoil he keeps or on the statement he gives the secretary. The letter gives one remittance for three members and leaves their individual amounts uncertain. It gives no separate record of payment by the bearer on his own account.¹⁶

The April papers concern the return of a receipt for the collection statement. On 15 April the treasurer asks for the receipt to be returned and filed with the April remittances, with the statement of sums provisional until it comes back.¹⁷ On 16 April he instructs the attendant to deliver the envelope containing the collection statement and supporting papers to the custodian at les Hautes Bornes. The custodian is to keep the envelope and issue a receipt. On 17 April the attendant records returning that receipt to the treasurer and notes that it was slipped into the remittances leaf.¹⁸ The recorded return of this receipt establishes a step in the custody of the papers. It belongs to a different transaction from the March remittance.

## 3. Guests and records of use

The October 1862 papers show the preparations for a Saturday reading. The secretary asks the attendant to arrange chairs and keep the passage from the entrance clear.¹⁹ On 27 October the attendant reports preparing the addresses and asks whether the letters should be sorted into two bundles, one for members of the district and one for the rest, before sealing.²⁰ The invitation of 28 October allows each member one companion and welcomes those who make no contribution towards heating.²¹ The duty instructions of 30 October place the attendant at the entrance to receive companions presented by members and show arrivals to their seats.²² Preparation, permission to enter and the work at the door can thus be described from different papers.

For the sitting on 8 November 1862 the secretary again allows each member one companion. The invitation is for that sitting and expressly excludes admission to membership through the invitation.²³ The postscript tells the attendant to list companions separately, to write the presenting member's name beside each guest and to leave the guests out of the membership count.²⁴ These instructions tell us who was invited and how the attendant was to enter the arrivals. An account of who came would require the entries themselves.

Two notices nearby concern meeting at the stone bridge at Clairpré. On 4 November 1862 the secretary gives ten o'clock as the hour and says that the courier from Bas-Maret is expected.²⁵ The correction of 6 November shifts the meeting to eleven and keeps the bridge as the rendezvous.²⁶ These notices establish an arrangement and a change of hour. I treat a rendezvous of this kind separately from the sittings for which we have invitations or instructions at the door.

A letter from the evening attendant to the secretary, dated 5 December 1865, gives a particular instance of reading-room use. The attendant entered the newspaper and book on two lines in the order requested because the objects differed. The same reader stayed in the room between the requests, so the attendant tells the secretary to count one visit for those two lines.²⁷ This explains how to count that pair of entries. A count across evenings would require evidence of how other repeated requests were entered and identified.

Earlier that year, on 7 January 1865, a duty instruction asked for the notebook and keys to be returned. The endorsement of 8 January records their return and states that nothing was added to the last page after closing.²⁸ On 9 January the outgoing attendant entrusted the notebook to his successor and proposed meeting the following day at la Combe aux Joncs for the remaining papers and a check of the keys. The incoming attendant acknowledges custody and confirms that the keys were checked, without naming where the check took place.²⁹ The January entries are evidence for the custody of the notebook. The December letter gives evidence of how a particular visit was recorded.

## 4. Deferment and permission

The January 1866 papers distinguish a member's request for time to pay from the committee's decision. The member asks the secretary to put the request before the committee.³⁰ A separate instruction asks the attendant to keep the room's papers together for the committee and to send nothing out before it decides how the registers are to be kept.³¹ On 29 January the secretary conveys the committee's decision to defer collection of the unpaid subscription until 12 February, with ordinary reminders to resume afterwards.³² He writes to the member on 30 January, and the treasurer reports entering the decision on the subscription statement on 3 February.³³ ³⁴ These are successive records of the communication and entry of the decision. The later collection of the subscription would require an entry of its own.

Other papers show how these decisions were conveyed and kept. A sealed notice was to be delivered at Val d’Erne and its duplicate retained with the collection papers.³⁵ The treasurer proposes leaving an envelope containing the deferments statement and notices at la Côte aux Ifs. The endorsement records the return of the statement, without giving a place for that return.³⁶

On 9 February 1866 the secretary tells the attendant that the committee has deferred collection in a particular case until the end of February. The person concerned may continue reading in the room. If he asks to take a volume away, the attendant is to record the request and refer it to the secretary before handing over the book.³⁷ The attendant is to keep the notice until month end and report any difficulty.³⁸ The duration applies to this deferment. Reading on the premises is expressly allowed, and an outside loan is subject to a separate procedure.

The stove correspondence gives a further reason to keep the accounts and the conditions of use apart. The secretary writes about the room's needs in cold weather.³⁹ A member with a subscription outstanding offers one franc for the stove and asks that it be kept off his subscription account.⁴⁰ The secretary says he will put the offer to the committee.⁴¹ The sequence takes us from a stated need to an offer and a referral.

The March papers follow a member's request to take a book home.⁴² In the secretary's instructions of 19 March 1866 the member may continue to read on the premises. The attendant is to collect the arrears before the book leaves the room and pass the money and the member's name to the treasurer.⁴³ The attendant reports leaving the book in the room.⁴⁴ On 26 March the member writes that he intends to come and consult the pages again. The end of the letter is partly illegible.⁴⁵ For this case we have the request, the terms given in reply and a report of where the book was left. A stated intention to return belongs in a different account from a visit recorded by the attendant.

## 5. Hours of opening

On 6 October 1868 the committee tells the Thursday attendants to try closing at eight instead of seven in the evening during October, with subscription terms unchanged. The register and keys are to pass at seven to the next attendant, who takes charge of the lighting.⁴⁶ The continuation directs that arrivals between seven and eight be entered separately, leaving out anyone already in the room at seven. The committee proposes to examine the journal at the end of October before deciding whether to maintain the hour.⁴⁷ The month and the two hours belong to the terms of the trial. The proposed review gives the reason for asking attendants to distinguish new arrivals from readers who stayed on.

The duty papers show the arrangements that made an evening's service possible. On 8 October an attendant leaves the key and notebook for his successor, gives the location of the lamp and asks him to return the notebook to the treasurer after the evening.⁴⁸ On 12 October the attendant reports bringing the treasurer a member's gift of one franc fifty centimes for the Thursday lamp and asks that it be kept apart until the expense is known.⁴⁹ On 13 October he asks for the unused seventy-five centimes so he can return them to the giver.⁵⁰ The treasurer replies on 15 October that the balance is with the attendant's notebook for collection on Thursday and has been kept apart from subscriptions.⁵¹ That is the last recorded step in the handling of the balance. On 16 October the attendant reports opening at the customary hour on Thursday and putting the lamp away after the reading. The report gives no closing time.⁵²

In the notice of 5 November 1868 the secretary tells the attendant to leave the key box at Brévalette for his successor to collect before opening, with the signed receipt to return separately.⁵³ On 19 November a notice specifies le Clos Nerlin as the temporary key deposit for the next sitting and corrects the announced opening from six to seven. Its endorsement records the return of the 5 November receipt.⁵⁴ Collecting the keys and opening to readers are separate acts. The November papers give instructions for particular sittings, while the evidence for the Thursday extension concerns the trial specified for October.

## 6. What survives

The catalogue of the Archives de Saint-Aubin-sur-Rive gives 235 numbered positions across boxes 12–14. Of these, 216 leaves survive and are available for copying and 19 positions are recorded as missing. Box 12 accounts for 72 surviving leaves and six missing positions, box 13 for 68 and five, and box 14 for 76 and eight. These figures describe the holdings. A reference within a surviving range gives a place to look for evidence.

The passages cited here are from 54 leaves I read in the paper copies at hand: ten from the first visit in July 2024, thirty-two from the September 2024 consignment and twelve received in January 2025. The citations identify the faces or passages used. Consecutive leaves may carry a single notice or document successive acts. The group on Thursday opening, for example, includes the committee's instructions, correspondence about the lamp and a report of an evening's work. Each speaks to a different part of the arrangements.

In the chapters that follow I compare provincial reading societies through the duties assigned to their officers, the stated conditions of admission and the identifiable acts that the records describe. A remittance can cover several members, a guest list can be prescribed for a particular sitting, and two entries in a register can record one visit. Before comparing how often people came, I would need to establish what the corresponding entries counted in each set of records.
