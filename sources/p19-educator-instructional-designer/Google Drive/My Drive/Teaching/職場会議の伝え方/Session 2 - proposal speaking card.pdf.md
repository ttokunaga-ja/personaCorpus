# Give the chair something to decide
Communicating in Workplace Meetings | ELM-CWM-2024-HO-002 | 19 Sep 2024 | Heather Callahan

## Start with the request
A speaker says: “I'd like to talk about Saturdays.” The chair can invite a discussion, but what exactly is the meeting being asked to decide?

Use item 4 of the maintenance agenda. There is a proposal to try Saturday mornings for two weeks, with at most six visits each Saturday. Item 3 reports that eight of 30 weekday repair visits in the last four weeks had to be rescheduled.

## Build a 30-second contribution
Write one short sentence in each box. Then say the three sentences aloud to a partner.

| Part | Your words |
|---|---|
| What I want the meeting to decide | |
| One fact that gives a reason | |
| A specific question to the chair that can be answered | |

For example: “I'd like us to decide whether to try Saturday mornings for two weeks, up to six visits each Saturday. Eight of the 30 weekday visits in the last four weeks had to be rescheduled. Chair, can we decide whether to try those two Saturdays?”

## Listen and revise
As the listener, mark the words where you can hear a question the meeting could decide. If you cannot find one, ask the speaker what decision they want. If a long account of the repairs comes first, point to one detail that could be left out while keeping the fact that supports the request.

Change places and try again. Keep the request clear; speaking faster is not the same as making the decision easier to hear.

Source: Rosedale Workers Housing Cooperative, Maintenance handover — operations meeting agenda extract, items 3 and 4.
