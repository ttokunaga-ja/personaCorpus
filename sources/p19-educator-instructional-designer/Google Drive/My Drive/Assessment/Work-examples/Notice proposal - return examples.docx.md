# Notice proposal: return examples

Building Proposals from Evidence | ELM-BPE-2024-IR-001 | v1.0 | 17 Jun 2024 | Heather Callahan

For Erin MacDonald and Heather Callahan

## At return

These are two anonymous proposals from 12 June. When we read the 14 paragraphs together on Friday, five used the number of stops as a count of people who read or could read the notice. The second example shows a different place to intervene: the writer uses the count carefully and then runs out of a way to check the proposed change. Use the matching prompt when we hand the drafts back. Leave both unscored.

## A clear request with a misstated count

> At lunchtime, in twenty minutes, nine people were able to read the notice. The sketch shows that the tall cabinet is in front of part of the notice on the north wall when someone comes in from the south door.

> Please move the notice permanently to the east wall so that people entering the room have a clearer view of it.

Source: *Notice move - first draft*, anonymous 12 June proposal.

You've given the room contact a specific request and used the sketch to point out where the cabinet might get in the way. Keep the east wall in your revision. Go back to the midday row and replace the opening with what the observer could tick off: “On 22 May, from 12:10 to 12:30, nine entries came to a complete stop at the south entrance observation line.” Add a sentence saying that the tally leaves open whether anyone read the notice. With that correction in place, look again at “permanently.” What could you ask the room contact to check before making that decision?

## A careful observation that needs a comparison

> During the twenty minutes at lunch there were nine stops at the entrance line. We do not know from that count whether the people who stopped read the notice.

> Before deciding to move it, I would want to know whether it is any easier to notice there. I am not sure how to check that against the present position.

Source: *Notice move - cautious draft*, anonymous 12 June proposal.

Keep the two sentences about the count. Your “might” also does useful work where you describe the cabinet: the sketch gives a reason to check the view, and the result is still open. Add a question the room contact could act on: “What could we observe in the same 20-minute window, from the same entrance line, with the same notice on the north wall one day and at the east-wall position on another?” Say exactly what you would record at both positions. If you choose complete stops, finish by saying what even a difference in the counts would leave unanswered about reading the notice.
